Spearman's ρ Monotonic relationships Ordinal scale sufficient Robust to outliers Less power than Pearson
Kendall's τ Small samples, ties Ordinal data Handles ties well Computationally intensive
Point-Biserial Binary vs continuous One dichotomous variable Tests group differences Limited to binary
Partial Correlation Controlling confounders Linear relationships Removes confounding Complex interpretation

🎯 Key Takeaways:

  • Correlation measures relationship strength: -1 to +1
  • Choose the right method: Pearson for linear, Spearman for monotonic
  • Correlation ≠ Causation: The golden rule of statistics
  • Always visualize: Scatter plots reveal patterns numbers miss
  • Check assumptions: Different methods have different requirements
  • Watch for spurious correlations: Confounding variables can deceive
  • Report confidence intervals: Point estimates aren't enough
  • Consider non-linear relationships: Not everything is a straight line

# BOOTSTRAP CONFIDENCE INTERVAL
def bootstrap_correlation(x, y, n_bootstrap=1000, ci=95):
    correlations = []
    n = len(x)
    for _ in range(n_bootstrap):
        idx = np.random.choice(n, n, replace=True)
        correlations.append(pearsonr(x[idx], y[idx])[0])
    return np.percentile(correlations, [(100-ci)/2, 100-(100-ci)/2])

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Correlation quantifies the strength and direction of a relationship from -1 to +1, but it only captures the kind of pattern the chosen method assumes.
  • Match the method to the data: Pearson for linear relationships, Spearman for monotonic ones, Kendall for small samples with ties, point-biserial for a binary variable.
  • Always visualize first — a scatter plot reveals non-linearity, outliers, and restricted range that a single coefficient hides.
  • Report confidence intervals and effect size (R²), correct for multiple comparisons, and never read causation into a correlation.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When should I use Spearman instead of Pearson?

Use Spearman when the relationship is monotonic but not linear, when your data are ordinal, or when outliers would distort Pearson. Spearman correlates the ranks rather than the raw values, making it more robust.

A correlation is strong and significant — does that mean one variable causes the other?

No. A strong, significant correlation can arise from a confounding variable, reverse causation, or coincidence. Establishing causation requires a controlled experiment or careful causal-inference methods.

What is a "spurious" correlation?

It's a correlation between two variables that are not directly related but move together because both are driven by a third factor (like ice-cream sales and drowning, both driven by summer). Partial correlation can help expose these.
