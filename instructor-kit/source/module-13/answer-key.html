4.6 · A/B Testing: self-check

Q1
Pinecrest showed the old and the new checkout page during the same four weeks and let a coin flip decide who saw which. What does the coin flip buy?
A) Two groups of exactly the same size, which the z-test requires
B) A smaller sample size than a before/after comparison would need
C) Groups that are alike in everything except the page, including things nobody measured
D) A guarantee that the difference between the pages is not due to chance
Why: Random assignment spreads every kind of visitor evenly across the arms, so the page is the only systematic difference. Chance is still possible; the test judges that.
A is wrong: The arms were 14,079 and 13,921. The test does not need equal sizes; it needs a split consistent with the design.
B is wrong: Randomization does not reduce the sample size. The power calculation sets it.
D is wrong: Randomization removes confounding, not chance. That is why a test and an interval are still needed.
Q2
A colleague sizes a test for a rise from 8.9% to 10.0% like this and gets 129,733 visitors per arm. What went wrong?
n = NormalIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=0.011, alpha=0.05, power=0.80)
A) effect_size expects Cohen's h from proportion_effectsize(0.100, 0.089), not the raw difference in rates
B) power should be 0.20, the chance of missing the effect
C) solve_power needs nobs1 as well, or it has nothing to solve
D) Nothing: a one-point lift really needs about 130,000 visitors per arm
Why: effect_size is Cohen's h (0.0376 here). Passing 0.011 asks for a much smaller effect than intended, and the answer is twelve times too large. The right call gives 11,092.
B is wrong: Power is the chance of detecting a real effect: 0.80 is the convention.
C is wrong: solve_power solves for the one argument you leave out. Giving all four raises ValueError: need exactly one keyword that is None.
D is wrong: With the effect size computed correctly the answer is 11,092 per arm.
Q3
Conversion went from 8.86% to 10.03%. What does this print? (First number: percentage points. Second: relative lift.)
print(round((0.1003 - 0.0886) * 100, 2), round(0.1003 / 0.0886 - 1, 3))
1.17 0.132
Why: 1.17 percentage points is the absolute difference. 0.132 (13.2%) is the relative lift: 1.17 is 13.2% of 8.86. Both are correct; always say which one you mean.
Q4
An experiment planned as 50/50 reports 14,400 visitors in treatment and 13,600 in control. stats.binomtest(14400, 28000, p=0.5).pvalue is 1.8e-06. What do you do?
A) Carry on: 51.4% is close enough to half
B) Drop 800 treatment visitors at random so the arms match, then analyze
C) Report the conversion result with a note that the split was uneven
D) Stop and find where the control visitors went: the experiment did not run as planned, so its results cannot be read
Why: A fair coin almost never gives 51.4% in 28,000 flips. This is a sample ratio mismatch: visitors are going missing from one arm, which breaks the comparison. Fix the bug and rerun.
A is wrong: With 28,000 visitors a fair coin stays very close to 50%; p = 0.0000018 says this is not chance.
B is wrong: Trimming hides the symptom. Whatever removed 800 control visitors probably removed a particular kind of visitor.
C is wrong: A failed health check is not a caveat to attach; nothing downstream can be trusted.
Q5
A team looks at its four-week test every Monday and stops the first time p is below 0.05. In the lesson's simulation of experiments with no real effect, how often did that rule declare a winner?
A) About 5%, because alpha is 0.05 at each look
B) About 12%, because each look is another chance to cross the line by luck
C) About 1.25%, because the four looks share the 5%
D) About 50%, because the p-value wanders at random
Why: One planned look kept false alarms near 5% (4.4% in the simulation). Stopping at any of four looks raised it to 12.2%. Peeking is the multiple-comparisons problem in disguise.
A is wrong: 5% holds for ONE look. Four chances to stop add up.
C is wrong: 1.25% is the stricter per-look threshold you would USE to pay for four looks, not the false-alarm rate of looking at 0.05.
D is wrong: The p-value wanders, but not that wildly: the simulated rate was 12.2% for weekly looks and about 27% for daily looks.
Q6
By device: mobile +1.76 points (p = 0.00004), desktop +0.27 points (p = 0.67). The test of the difference between the two lifts gives p = 0.049, and nobody planned this comparison. Which sentence belongs in the memo?
A) The new page works on mobile and does nothing on desktop
B) The new page should be shipped to mobile visitors only
C) The gain appears concentrated on mobile; the evidence that devices differ is borderline and exploratory, and worth a follow-up test
D) The desktop result proves the page has no effect on desktop
Why: "Significant here, not there" is not evidence that here and there differ. The direct test of the difference is on the line (p = 0.049) and unplanned, so it is a lead to confirm.
A is wrong: Desktop's interval runs from about -1.0 to +1.5 points: a lift as large as the overall 1.17 is still inside it.
B is wrong: That builds a strategy on an unplanned, borderline comparison. The confirmed result is the overall lift.
D is wrong: Not significant means not detected. It never proves zero.

4.6 · Predict, then run

P1
Output and reasoning
print(round(proportions_ztest([20, 10], [100, 100])[1], 3))
Answer: 0.048. Just below 0.05 (p = 0.048), even though one rate is double the other. With 100 visitors per arm the interval is wide; "twice as good" on small counts is weak evidence.
P2
Output and reasoning
h = proportion_effectsize(0.100, 0.089)
n90 = NormalIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=h, alpha=0.05, power=0.90)
print(round(n90))
Answer: 14850. About 14,850 per arm: a third more visitors than the 11,092 needed for 80% power. Power is bought with sample size.
P3
Output and reasoning
print(round(stats.binomtest(5100, 10_000, p=0.5).pvalue, 3))
print(round(stats.binomtest(5080, 10_000, p=0.5).pvalue, 3))
Answer: 0.047 / 0.112. 5,100 / 4,900 fails (p = 0.047); 5,080 / 4,920 passes (p = 0.112). A 51% share looks harmless and is already unusual for a fair coin at this size. Many teams use a stricter threshold (0.01 or 0.001) for this check so that it does not cry wolf.

4.7 · An EDA Workflow: self-check

Q7
Someone hands you a table you have never seen. After writing your questions, what is the first fact to establish about it?
A) Which columns correlate most strongly
B) Its grain: what one row represents, and which column identifies a row
C) Whether the numeric columns are normally distributed
D) Which rows are outliers, so they can be removed
Why: Every count and every average depends on what one row is. Structure (grain, keys, types) is step 2 of the routine, straight after the questions.
A is wrong: Pairs of columns are step 5. Without the grain you cannot say what a correlation is a correlation of.
C is wrong: Shape matters in step 4, and only for the columns your questions need.
D is wrong: Unusual rows are investigated in step 3, not removed on sight. Pinecrest's twelve bulk orders are real.
Q8
What does this print?
df = pd.DataFrame({"a": [1.0, None, 3.0],
                   "b": [None, 5.0, 6.0],
                   "c": ["x", "y", None]})
print(len(df.dropna()), len(df.dropna(subset=["a"])))
0 2
Why: Every row has a gap somewhere, so dropna() with no arguments keeps nothing. Naming the one column the analysis needs keeps the two rows where a is present.
Q9
In the survey, sat_website is missing for 46% of store shoppers and 3% of online shoppers. Its mean is 3.54. What is the most accurate description?
A) Pinecrest customers rate the website 3.54
B) The mean is unusable until the gaps are filled with 3.54
C) Store shoppers rate the website 3.54
D) Customers who answered, mostly online shoppers, rate the website 3.54
Why: The values are not missing at random: people skipped the question about the part of Pinecrest they do not use. The column describes those who answered.
A is wrong: That claims to speak for everyone, including the many store shoppers who never answered.
B is wrong: Filling with the mean invents opinions. Leave missing values missing and say who answered.
C is wrong: It is the other way round: store shoppers are the ones who mostly skipped it.
Q10
What does this print?
left = pd.DataFrame({"id": [1, 2, 3]})
right = pd.DataFrame({"id": [1, 1, 1, 2], "x": [5, 6, 7, 8]})
print(len(left.merge(right, on="id")),
      len(left.merge(right, on="id", how="left")))
4 5
Why: id 1 matches three rows and id 2 matches one: 4 rows in the inner merge, and id 3 is dropped. The left merge keeps id 3 with a missing x: 5 rows. Three rows became four or five without any message, which is why merges get validate=.
Q11
You compare online order totals across 22 groups (weekdays, payment methods, months). Three groups have p below 0.05; after a Holm correction one remains. What goes in the findings?
A) The one that survived, with the size of its gap, the number of comparisons, and the result of checking an obvious competing explanation; the other two are leads
B) All three, with their original p-values
C) None: results found by scanning are never reportable
D) Only the group with the largest gap in dollars, whatever its p-value
Why: Scanning is multiple testing. Correct for it, judge the size of the gap, challenge the survivor, and say that 22 comparisons were made. Uncorrected hits are leads for later.
B is wrong: With 22 tests about one false alarm is expected at 0.05. Uncorrected p-values overstate the evidence.
C is wrong: Exploration is how leads are found. A corrected, sanity-checked result can be reported as exploratory.
D is wrong: The largest gap may come from the smallest, noisiest group.
Q12
Which sentence from the survey note is a caveat, not a finding?
A) Only 42% of customers are satisfied with prices
B) Every respondent has placed an order, so customers who never ordered are not heard
C) Asheville's staff rating is 3.62 against about 4.1 at the other stores
D) Asheville is the only store whose revenue fell in 2025
Why: A caveat says who or what the data cannot speak for. This one limits every finding in the note to active customers.
A is wrong: That is a finding: one sentence with a number.
C is wrong: A finding (and one that survived the correction for 55 comparisons).
D is wrong: A finding from a second dataset that points the same way.

4.7 · Predict, then run

P1
Output and reasoning
left = pd.DataFrame({"k": [1, 1, 2]})
right = pd.DataFrame({"k": [1, 1, 2], "v": [10, 20, 30]})
print(len(left.merge(right, on="k")))
Answer: 5. 5 rows from 3: the key 1 appears twice on each side, so it matches 2 × 2 = 4 times, plus one row for key 2. A many-to-many merge multiplies. validate="one_to_one" or "many_to_one" would have raised an error.
P2
Output and reasoning
s = pd.Series([4, None, 2])
print(s.mean(), s.fillna(0).mean())
Answer: 3.0 2.0. mean() skips the gap: (4 + 2) / 2 = 3.0 is the honest mean. fillna(0) counts the missing answer as a zero and gives 2.0.
P3
Output and reasoning
print(multipletests([0.01, 0.04, 0.03], method="holm")[1].round(2).tolist())
Answer: [0.03, 0.06, 0.06]. One. Holm multiplies the smallest p by 3 (0.03), the next by 2 (0.03 × 2 = 0.06) and keeps the last at least as large as the one before (0.06). Three raw hits become one.

Warm-up and Exit Ticket

Warm-up: retrieval from Session 12
  1. Across store-days, staff on duty and revenue correlate at r = 0.88. Give one reason this does not mean "add staff, sell more". Answer: Staff are scheduled from expected demand (weekday, season, promotions), and expected demand also drives revenue: a confounder. Comparing like with like (same store, month, weekday, promo status) the correlation falls to 0.14.
  2. For the same two columns Pearson's r is -0.32 and Spearman's is -0.61. What does the gap tell you? Answer: The relationship is consistent in direction but curved (or one influential point is distorting Pearson). Spearman works on ranks, so it measures any monotonic trend. Plot it.
  3. Finish the sentence: "A p-value is the probability of ... assuming ..." Answer: ...a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is not the probability that the null is true, and it says nothing about the size of the effect.
  4. Which test: (a) the mean of two independent groups of different sizes; (b) whether two categorical columns are related? Answer: (a) Welch's t-test, stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var=False). (b) Chi-square on a table of counts, stats.chi2_contingency(pd.crosstab(a, b)). Hold on to (b): today's first test is the same test in another form.
  5. You run 20 tests at alpha = 0.05 and one comes back with p = 0.04. What do you say about it? Answer: About one false alarm is expected from 20 tests when nothing is going on. Count every test, correct (Holm), and call a scanned result exploratory.
Exit ticket
  1. Name two things you check about an A/B test before you look at a single conversion. Answer: Any two of: one row per unit (visitor_id is unique); the split is consistent with the design (stats.binomtest against 50/50: here p = 0.35); the arms are balanced on things fixed before the page was shown (device, returning). If a check fails: stop, do not adjust.
  2. "Significant on mobile, not significant on desktop." What is the right next step, and what word goes in the memo? Answer: Test the difference between the two lifts directly (here 1.49 points, p = 0.049) and correct for the slices. The word is "exploratory": the comparison was not planned.
  3. Write the first three steps of the EDA routine and say what "grain" means. Answer: Questions, structure, quality (then one variable, two variables, segments, findings and caveats). Grain: what one row of the table represents.
Common wrong answers and what they reveal
  • "A p-value is the chance the result is due to chance" (warm-up 3). The learner has the conditional backward. They will misread peeking ("p was 0.03, so 97% sure") and scans. Have them say the full sentence with "assuming the null is true" once per session until it sticks.
  • "The arms must be equal in size" (Q1, exit 1). Confuses balance with equality. The check is against what a fair coin produces, not against 50.00%.
  • Mixing points and percent (Q3). Writing "conversion rose 1.17%" or "13 points". Reveals that the learner computes without saying units; insist on the unit in every spoken answer today.
  • "Not significant, so no effect" (Q6, lab A). The oldest mistake of Module 4. Ask for the interval: desktop's runs from -0.97 to +1.50 points.
  • "Fill the gaps with the mean" (Q9). The learner treats missing values as a nuisance to remove, not as information about who answered.
  • A finding offered as a caveat or the reverse (Q12, exit 3). Usually a sign that the note was written as a diary of steps. The F / C / N marking exercise from the lab debrief fixes it.

Equally correct variations. Mann-Whitney instead of Welch (the distribution is close to symmetric, so both fit). Medians instead of means ($44.91 against $41.52). The ratio of sums instead of the mean of daily ratios ($45.06 against $41.53, +8.5%): say which one was used. A bootstrap interval. A bar chart of twelve months instead of lines. Excluding the promo days at the start of December changes nothing visible. Any of these with a clear note is Proficient or better.

What separates Proficient from Exemplary here
  • The winter plateau. "The rest of the year" contains November ($42.56), January ($43.55) and February ($43.23). Against March to October ($41.12) the December gap is $3.84 (+9.3%), not $3.31. Seeing this means the learner looked at every month before trusting a two-group split.
  • The wording about cause. The data show that revenue per unit is higher. "Shoppers buy pricier things" is one explanation; higher winter prices is another; this file cannot separate them. An Exemplary note says so and names the files that could (order_items, products).
  • Independence. Store-days close together are alike, so 238 December rows are not 238 independent observations and the p-value is too small. With d = 1.33 in every store and both years the conclusion does not depend on it; saying so is the mark of someone who read Lesson 4.5's caveat about days.
Common weak answers and what they reveal
  • "December is $44.95, the rest is $41.65, so yes." Two means and no uncertainty: Analysis at level 1. Ask: "How much would those two numbers move with a different set of days?" The interval ($2.97 to $3.65) answers that in one line.
  • "p = 2.0e-52, so December shoppers definitely buy more expensive things." A tiny p-value read as a large, causal fact. The p-value says the gap is not chance; the size is the $3.31 (+7.9%); and "buy more expensive things" is one explanation. Higher prices in winter would produce the same column. Communication at level 2.
  • A NaN result, or "the mean is nan". The 6 missing revenue values were passed to NumPy or SciPy (stats.ttest_ind returns nan when the input holds NaN). Lesson 4.5, Common Mistake 1. Data handling at level 1 or 2; the fix is dropna(subset=[...]) and a sentence saying so.
  • Total December revenue compared with average monthly revenue. Answers a different question (December is the busiest month) with a different measure. The plan row decides this: the question names revenue ÷ units.
  • A chi-square test, or a paired t-test. Chi-square is for counts in categories; a paired test needs pairs, and December days have no partner days. Welch's t-test or Mann-Whitney fits two independent groups of a numeric measure.
  • "December is higher than the rest of the year" and nothing about the other winter months. Correct and still Proficient. Exemplary work plots or tabulates all twelve months and notices that January ($43.55), February ($43.23) and November ($42.56) sit in "the rest" and pull it up: against March to October the gap is $3.84, not $3.31.
  • Twelve separate month-against-rest tests with no correction, then "four months are significant". A scan presented as planned results. Not wrong to look, but it needs the count of tests and a correction (Lesson 4.5), or one planned comparison plus a descriptive monthly table.
