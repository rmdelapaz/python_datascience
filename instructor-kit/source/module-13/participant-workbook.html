4.6

A/B Testing

Key ideas
  • Why experiment? In observational data the groups you compare differ in more ways than the one you care about. Random assignment makes the groups alike in everything except the treatment, including things nobody measured. That is what lets you use the word "caused".
  • Plan before data. Six lines, written first: the unit (what is randomized and counted), the primary metric (the one number that decides), the guardrail (what must not get worse), the hypotheses (two-sided, alpha 0.05), the sample size and duration, and the decision rule.
  • Sample size comes from four ingredients: the baseline rate, the smallest lift worth catching, alpha and power. proportion_effectsize(target, baseline) turns the two rates into Cohen's h; NormalIndPower().solve_power(...) finds whichever one of effect size, sample size, alpha and power you leave out. Small lifts on low rates need many visitors: 8.9% to 10.0% needs 11,092 per arm.
  • Check the experiment before the result: one row per unit; a split consistent with the design (stats.binomtest against 50/50); arms balanced on things fixed in advance. A failed check means stop and find the bug, not adjust.
  • Report four things: the difference in percentage points, its 95% confidence interval, the relative lift (and say which is which), and the p-value. Then lay the interval next to what is worth having.
  • Peeking (stopping the first time p dips below 0.05) is multiple testing in disguise: false alarms rise from 5% to about 12% with weekly looks and past 25% with daily looks. Fix the sample size and decide once.
  • Segments are exploratory. "Significant here, not there" does not show that here and there differ. Test the difference between the two lifts, correct for the slices, and label unplanned results.
  • Judge on every randomized visitor. Buyers are not a randomized group, because the page helps decide who buys. Revenue per visitor (a non-buyer counts as $0) keeps the protection of the coin flip.

Worked example: does the new page work better on weekends?

A colleague says weekend shoppers are in less of a hurry, so the new page "must matter more on weekends". Check it the way the lesson checks devices: compare each slice, then test the difference between the lifts. (ab and compare_rates are the lesson's.)

ab["weekend"] = ab["date"].dt.dayofweek >= 5
weekday = compare_rates(ab[~ab["weekend"]])
weekend = compare_rates(ab[ab["weekend"]])
for label, r in [("Mon-Fri", weekday), ("Sat-Sun", weekend)]:
    print(f"{label}: {r['visitors']:>6,} visitors  "
          f"{r['control']:.2%} -> {r['treatment']:.2%}  {r['diff'] * 100:+.2f} pts "
          f"(95% CI {r['ci_low'] * 100:+.2f} to {r['ci_high'] * 100:+.2f})  "
          f"p = {r['p']:.3f}")

gap = weekend["diff"] - weekday["diff"]
gap_se = np.sqrt(weekend["se"] ** 2 + weekday["se"] ** 2)
p_gap = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(gap / gap_se))
print(f"Weekend lift minus weekday lift: {gap * 100:+.2f} points, p = {p_gap:.2f}")
Mon-Fri: 20,082 visitors  8.88% -> 9.91%  +1.03 pts (95% CI +0.22 to +1.84)  p = 0.012
Sat-Sun:  7,918 visitors  8.80% -> 10.33%  +1.53 pts (95% CI +0.23 to +2.83)  p = 0.021
Weekend lift minus weekday lift: +0.50 points, p = 0.52

Reading it. Both slices show a lift, and both are significant by themselves. The weekend lift looks larger (+1.53 against +1.03 points), but the difference between them is half a point with p = 0.52: no evidence that the page works differently on weekends. Notice the weekend interval is wider. There are fewer weekend visitors, so the estimate is rougher. Nobody planned this comparison, so the sentence for a memo is: "The lift is similar on weekdays and weekends (exploratory)."

4.7

An EDA Workflow

Key ideas
  • EDA is a routine, not "make charts until something looks interesting": 1 Questions · 2 Structure · 3 Quality · 4 One variable · 5 Two variables · 6 Segments · 7 Findings and caveats, and then around again with the new questions.
  • Questions come first. They decide which columns matter, which comparisons are planned and which are fishing, and when you are done. Changing a question openly is fine; inventing one afterward to fit a pretty chart is not.
  • Know the grain: what one row is, and which column identifies it. Every count and every average depends on it. Make merges prove the relationship you expect with validate=.
  • For missing data ask "missing for whom?" Values that are not missing at random change who a column describes. Decide per question; dropna(subset=[...]), never a blanket dropna().
  • Ask who is in the data compared with who you want to talk about. The survey speaks for active, fairly loyal customers.
  • Look before you summarize: every distribution before its mean, the grouped values behind every correlation.
  • Scanning segments is multiple testing. Two disciplines: (1) count every comparison and correct (Holm); (2) for what survives, look at the size of the gap and check the obvious competing explanation.
  • An analysis ends in writing: findings (one sentence with a number each), caveats (who and what the data cannot speak for), next questions.

Worked example: do frequent buyers recommend Pinecrest more?

A new question for the survey, and a merge that would go wrong if you were careless. orders has one row per order, the survey one row per customer. Merging them directly would repeat each response once per order (1,800 rows become 9,230). So summarize the orders to one row per customer first, then merge, and let validate prove it. (survey is the lesson's.)

orders = pd.read_csv("data/orders.csv")
per_customer = (orders.groupby("customer_id")
                      .agg(n_orders=("order_id", "count"))
                      .reset_index())
print("orders:", len(orders), "rows -> one row per customer:", len(per_customer))

joined = survey.merge(per_customer, on="customer_id", how="left",
                      validate="one_to_one")
print("survey rows before:", len(survey), "after:", len(joined),
      "| no orders found:", joined["n_orders"].isna().sum())

rho = joined["n_orders"].corr(joined["recommend"], method="spearman")
print(f"Spearman correlation, number of orders and recommend score: {rho:.2f}")
joined["buyer"] = pd.cut(joined["n_orders"], [0, 2, 5, 10, 100],
                         labels=["1-2", "3-5", "6-10", "11+"])
by_buyer = joined.groupby("buyer", observed=True)["recommend"]
print(by_buyer.agg(["count", "median", "mean"]).round(2))

Reading it. The merge kept 1,800 rows and every respondent has orders (step 3 of the routine already said so). The correlation is -0.02: no relationship. The grouped values agree: the mean recommend score is between 7.2 and 7.5 whether someone has ordered twice or a dozen times. An honest finding: "Among survey respondents, how often a customer orders is unrelated to how likely they are to recommend Pinecrest (Spearman -0.02)." A caveat: respondents are all active customers, so this says nothing about people who stopped buying. A null result, checked and written down, is a result.

Glossary

A/B test: an experiment that shows two versions to randomly assigned groups at the same time and compares one planned metric. Arm: one of the groups in an experiment (control, treatment). Unit: what is randomized and counted: here, one visitor. Primary metric: the single number, chosen in advance, that decides the test. Guardrail metric: a number that must not get worse while the primary metric improves. Minimum detectable effect: the smallest improvement the test is sized to have a good chance of catching. Power: the chance of detecting an effect of a given size if it is real (0.80 by convention). Cohen's h: the effect size for a difference between two proportions; what statsmodels power functions expect. Sample ratio mismatch: a split between arms that differs from the design by more than chance allows; a sign of a bug. Percentage points: the absolute difference between two rates (10.03% - 8.86% = 1.17 points). Relative lift: the difference as a share of the baseline (1.17 / 8.86 = 13.2%). Peeking: checking a running test and stopping when it first looks significant; inflates false alarms. Exploratory: found by looking, not planned in advance; a lead to confirm, not a confirmed result. EDA: exploratory data analysis: getting to know a dataset before relying on it. Grain: what one row of a table represents. Key: the column (or columns) whose values identify a row. Missing not at random: gaps that are more common for some kinds of rows, so the column describes only those who answered. Holm correction: an adjustment to p-values that accounts for having run several tests. Caveat: a specific statement of who or what the data cannot speak for.
