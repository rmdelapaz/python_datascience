Session 13: A/B Testing and the EDA Workflow

How to teach the session that closes Module 4: one real experiment from the plan written before launch to the ship decision, a seven-step routine for data nobody has explained to you, and a 30-minute checkpoint in which learners run that routine alone.

How to Use This Guide

  • This is the last session of Module 4 and the fullest of the course: two lessons and Checkpoint 1 in 180 minutes. The lesson blocks are 60 and 55 minutes instead of the usual 75 to 80. The guide says exactly what is typed in the room, what is shown from the lesson page, and what goes home.
  • Same rhythm as every session, compressed: Frame (goal and mental model) → Live demo (you type, they follow) → Guided lab (new practice from the kit) → Start the project (five minutes: the brief and the first cell). Then the checkpoint.
  • Type, don't paste, with one stated exception (the 55-test scan in the 4.7 demo). Every code block below was run on the course data; the gray block under it is the real output.
  • Labs for this session live in labs/module-13/: starters for learners, solutions for you. Teal blocks are hands-on, amber blocks are watch-outs.
  • Keep statistics_ab_testing.html and statistics_eda_workflow.html open in browser tabs for the diagrams and the three charts you show but do not type (the daily p-value chart, the six bar panels, the heatmap of gaps).
The #1 risk of Session 13: the clock

The lesson pages estimate 100 + 110 = 210 minutes for a solo learner. After the warm-up, the break, the checkpoint and the wrap-up the room has 115. The checkpoint is fixed at 2:15: do not let the EDA lab run into it. If you are behind at the break, shorten the 4.7 demo (see "If You Run Long"), never the checkpoint. The second risk is tone: a checkpoint in the last hour makes some learners tense from 0:00. Say in the first minute that it is practice for the capstone, that it is open notebook, and that it is not graded.

Known environment traps

The course pins 0.15: python -m pip install -r requirements.txt inside the course environment fixes it. Wrong working folder. FileNotFoundError: data/ab_test.csv means the notebook is not next to the data/ folder; the labs do not have this problem (they find the data through labs_paths). A chart window that blocks. In a plain terminal plt.show() waits for the window to close; in the checkpoint tell terminal users to savefig instead. NaN from SciPy. stats.ttest_ind returns nan if either input contains a missing value: today that is a learning moment in the checkpoint, not an environment fault. Do not fix it for them.

Five retrieval questions (Lessons 4.4 and 4.5)
  1. Across store-days, staff on duty and revenue correlate at r = 0.88. Give one reason this does not mean "add staff, sell more". Answer: Staff are scheduled from expected demand (weekday, season, promotions), and expected demand also drives revenue: a confounder. Comparing like with like (same store, month, weekday, promo status) the correlation falls to 0.14.
  2. For the same two columns Pearson's r is -0.32 and Spearman's is -0.61. What does the gap tell you? Answer: The relationship is consistent in direction but curved (or one influential point is distorting Pearson). Spearman works on ranks, so it measures any monotonic trend. Plot it.
  3. Finish the sentence: "A p-value is the probability of ... assuming ..." Answer: ...a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is not the probability that the null is true, and it says nothing about the size of the effect.
  4. Which test: (a) the mean of two independent groups of different sizes; (b) whether two categorical columns are related? Answer: (a) Welch's t-test, stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var=False). (b) Chi-square on a table of counts, stats.chi2_contingency(pd.crosstab(a, b)). Hold on to (b): today's first test is the same test in another form.
  5. You run 20 tests at alpha = 0.05 and one comes back with p = 0.04. What do you say about it? Answer: About one false alarm is expected from 20 tests when nothing is going on. Count every test, correct (Holm), and call a scanned result exploratory.

A/B Testing

Objectives (from the lesson page)

Mental model to say

"Imagine sorting a crowd into two rooms by flipping a coin for each person. Tall people, impatient people, people on phones, people who were going to buy anyway: every kind ends up split about evenly, including kinds you never thought to measure. The rooms are alike in everything except what you do to them next. So if they behave differently afterward, either the treatment did it or chance did, and last session taught you how to judge chance."

Bridge from Session 12: "Last time rain and visitors: we had to work hard to argue that rain itself was responsible, because rain comes with seasons. An experiment removes that weakness by design. It does not make the statistics optional. Most of what goes wrong in A/B testing goes wrong AROUND the test."

Then put the six-line plan on the board and leave it there all session: Unit (a visitor who reaches checkout) · Primary metric (conversion per visitor) · Guardrail (order value, revenue per visitor) · Hypotheses (two-sided, alpha 0.05) · Sample size and duration (computed, then fixed) · Decision rule (ship if conversion is significantly higher and the guardrail holds). Ask: "Why two-sided when everyone hopes the page is better?" (A new page can be worse, and you would need to know.)

Live demo (you type, they follow): the checkout experiment, plan to decision

One notebook, session13_ab.ipynb, in the folder that holds data/. Twelve short cells. The clock is tight: the marked minutes add up to 25. If you are two minutes behind at step 6, say steps 2 and 9 instead of typing them.

  1. Imports and the sample size (3m). Say: "Before any data exists, four ingredients: the baseline, the smallest lift worth catching, alpha and power. statsmodels wants the two rates as ONE number, Cohen's h." Type the imports once; they serve the whole demo.

  8. Wrap it in a function (2m). Say: "We will run this comparison on slices all afternoon. One function, one dictionary of everything worth reporting." Type it carefully; the lab and the project reuse it. 
    def compare_rates(df):
    """Treatment vs control conversion for the rows in df."""
    s = df.groupby("variant")["converted"].agg(["sum", "count"])
    c_c, n_c = s.loc["control"]
    c_t, n_t = s.loc["treatment"]
    r_c, r_t = c_c / n_c, c_t / n_t
    ci = confint_proportions_2indep(c_t, n_t, c_c, n_c, compare="diff")
    return {"visitors": int(n_c + n_t), "control": r_c, "treatment": r_t,
            "diff": r_t - r_c, "ci_low": ci[0], "ci_high": ci[1],
            "se": np.sqrt(r_c * (1 - r_c) / n_c + r_t * (1 - r_t) / n_t),
            "p": proportions_ztest([c_t, c_c], [n_t, n_c])[1]}

overall = compare_rates(ab)
for name in ["control", "treatment", "diff", "ci_low", "ci_high", "p"]:

Three mistakes to expect
  • The raw difference as the effect size. Someone types solve_power(effect_size=0.011, ...) and gets 129,733 per arm. No error. Coach: "What does the documentation call that argument? Which function turns two rates into it?" (proportion_effectsize(target, baseline); the right answer is 11,092.) In the lab this shows up as a follow-up test that would take a year.
  • Rates instead of counts into the z-test. proportions_ztest([0.1003, 0.0886], [14079, 13921]) prints z = 0.024, p = 0.98. Coach: "Does your result reproduce the rates you printed a cell ago? What would 'a tenth of one conversion' mean?" The habit to teach: counts in, and check the output against the summary table.
  • Two p-values compared to declare a difference. "Week 4 is significant and weeks 1 to 3 are not, so it only worked in week 4." Coach: "Read me the four lifts, not the four p-values. Does any weekly interval exclude +1.17?" Then: "A claim about a difference needs a test of the difference." The lab is built on exactly this.
Guided lab (20 min)

Learners open labs/module-13/l4_6-ab-testing/novelty_check_starter.py; your reference is labs/module-13/l4_6-ab-testing/novelty_check_solution.py. The question is new: a product manager fears a novelty effect (a lift that was big in week 1 and has been shrinking), and a colleague reads the weekly table as "it only worked in week 4". compare_rates and describe are given.

  • Minutes 0 to 5, TODO 1 and 2. Walk the room. The week column is the demo's line; the trap is using <= (cumulative, the peeking table) instead of == (each week by itself). Expected weekly lifts: +1.05, +0.76, +0.80, +2.08 points; only week 4 has p below 0.05 (0.0026).
  • Minutes 5 to 12, TODO 3. Halves: +0.91 points (weeks 1 to 2) and +1.43 (weeks 3 to 4). Difference +0.52 points, standard error 0.70, p = 0.46. If someone subtracts the p-values, send them back to the demo's device cell.
  • Minutes 12 to 17, TODO 4. Everyone writes the sentence. Ask two learners to read theirs aloud. A good one: "Every week shows a positive lift and every weekly interval contains the overall +1.17. The late lift is not smaller than the early one (p = 0.46 for the difference), so there is no sign of a novelty effect. One week alone is too small to be significant."
  • Minutes 17 to 20, debrief. Two questions: "Why is week 4 significant alone and week 2 not?" (Chance plus 7,000 visitors per week: a week has little power.) "Was this check in the plan?" (No: exploratory, and say so.)

    from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests

rows = []
for seg in ["store", "age_group"]:
    for level in sorted(survey[seg].unique()):
        inside = survey[seg] == level
        for item in items:
            x = survey.loc[inside, item].dropna()
            y = survey.loc[~inside, item].dropna()
            rows.append({"group": level, "item": item, "n": len(x),
                         "gap": x.mean() - y.mean(),
                         "p": stats.mannwhitneyu(x, y).pvalue})
scan = pd.DataFrame(rows)
scan["p_holm"] = multipletests(scan["p"], method="holm")[1]
print(f"{len(scan)} tests, {(scan['p'] < 0.05).sum()} below 0.05 before "
      f"correction, {(scan['p_holm'] < 0.05).sum()} after")

Three mistakes to expect
  • dropna() on the whole table. In the lab or the checkpoint someone drops every row with any gap. On the survey that leaves 295 of 1,800 responses. Coach: "How many rows did you have before and after that line? Which column does THIS question need?" (dropna(subset=[...]).)
  • A merge that multiplies rows. survey.merge(orders, on='customer_id') turns 1,800 rows into 9,230 and no message appears. Coach: "What is one row now? Say it out loud." Then: "What relationship did you expect? Put it in validate= and let pandas check." Summarize to one row per customer first, then merge.
  • The best of many comparisons reported as if it were planned. Learners will scan a table, find the biggest gap and write it up with its raw p-value. Coach: "How many groups did you look at before you chose that one?" The three-part fix: count, correct, call it exploratory, and look at the size of the gap, not only its p-value.
