4.4 · Correlation Analysis: self-check

Q1
Across the 234 products that have sold, the number of reviews and the units sold have r = 0.46. Which reading is soundest?
A) Asking customers for more reviews will raise sales
B) Products with more reviews tend to have sold more; the arrow may run from sales to reviews, and the data alone cannot say
C) 46% of a product's sales are explained by its reviews
D) The correlation is too weak to mean anything
Why: A correlation describes how two columns move together. Every sale is a chance to leave a review, so the arrow can run backward; only an experiment (asking for reviews on some products and not others) could show that reviews cause sales.
A is wrong: That reads the correlation as a cause, in one chosen direction.
C is wrong: r is not a percentage. The share of variance a line accounts for is r squared: about 20% here.
D is wrong: 0.46 is a moderate, clearly visible association. Weak is not the same as meaningless.
Q2
What does this print?
x = pd.Series([1, 2, 3, 4])
y = pd.Series([10, 8, 6, 4])
print(round(x.corr(y), 2))
-1.0
Why: Every step up in x is exactly a step of 2 down in y: all points lie on one falling straight line, so r is -1. r measures how tightly points hug a line, not how steep it is (a slope of -2 still gives -1).
Q3
To check whether staffing and revenue are linked beyond the calendar, which calculation compares like with like?
A) Correlate the z-scores of staff and revenue instead of the raw values
B) Use Spearman's correlation instead of Pearson's
C) Subtract each group's mean (same store, month, weekday and promotion status) from both columns with groupby().transform("mean"), then correlate the deviations
D) Remove the outlier days first, then recompute r
Why: Deviations from comparable days remove what the calendar explains, so what is left compares days that a manager expected to be alike. In the lesson r falls from 0.88 to 0.14.
A is wrong: r is already built from z-scores; standardizing does not change it.
B is wrong: Spearman handles curves and outliers. It does not remove a confounder.
D is wrong: The busy days are real and are exactly where the confounder acts; dropping them does not compare like with like.
Q4
For which pair of columns is Spearman clearly the better choice than Pearson?
A) Visitors and revenue, which form a straight, tight band
B) Revenue in dollars and the same revenue in cents
C) Two columns that are identical
D) 1-to-5 overall satisfaction and the 0-to-10 recommend score
Why: Survey answers are ordinal: ordered, without guaranteed equal steps. Spearman works on ranks, so it needs only the order. (The lesson gets 0.63 for this pair, from 1,699 people who answered both.)
A is wrong: A straight band without extreme points is exactly Pearson's home ground.
B is wrong: A change of units does not change r at all, by either method.
C is wrong: Both methods give exactly 1.
Q5
A line fitted to two columns with r = 0.5. What does this print?
r = 0.5
print(f"the line accounts for {r ** 2:.0%} of the variance")
the line accounts for 25% of the variance
Why: The share of variance a straight line accounts for is r squared. r = 0.5 sounds like "halfway" and accounts for a quarter; r = 0.3 leaves more than 90% unexplained.
Q6
A regression of revenue on staff on duty gives a slope of $1,154 per extra staff member. A manager plans to add two people every Tuesday to gain about $2,300 a day. What is the problem?
A) A slope describes how revenue differs across days with different staffing, not what changing the staffing would do; here it is mostly the calendar acting on both
B) The slope must first be multiplied by r
C) The intercept was left out of the calculation
D) Regression cannot be used when one column holds whole numbers
Why: Staff are scheduled from expected demand, which also drives revenue. Among comparable days the correlation falls to 0.14, and holding visitors constant to 0.07. The slope is a correct description and a poor forecast of an intervention.
B is wrong: A slope is already in dollars per staff member; multiplying by r has no meaning.
C is wrong: For a change of two staff the intercept cancels: only the slope matters. The problem is causal, not arithmetic.
D is wrong: Whole-number predictors are fine for a least-squares line.

4.5 · Hypothesis Testing: self-check

Q7
Shuffling the store labels 10,000 times gives a p-value of 0.030 for Asheville's lower recommend score. What does 0.030 measure?
A) In a world where the store makes no difference, about 3% of shuffles produced a gap at least as large as the real one
B) There is a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true
C) There is a 97% probability that Asheville really is worse
D) The gap is 3% of the average score
Why: A p-value is computed by assuming the null is true: it describes how often chance alone gives data at least this extreme. It is not the probability of any hypothesis.
B is wrong: The p-value assumes the null; it cannot also be the probability that the null is true.
C is wrong: The same mistake turned around. A test does not give the probability that a hypothesis is true.
D is wrong: A p-value is not a size. The gap is 0.36 points; its effect size is d = -0.17.
Q8
You want to compare 1-to-5 staff-satisfaction ratings at Asheville (193 answers) with the other stores (754 answers). Which test fits best?
A) A Welch t-test on the two means
B) A paired t-test
C) A one-sample t-test against 4
D) A Mann-Whitney U test
Why: Ratings are ordinal, so a rank-based test for two independent groups is the honest choice. Report the probability of superiority, U / (n1 x n2), as the effect size (0.37 in the lesson).
A is wrong: A mean assumes the step from 1 to 2 equals the step from 4 to 5; for ratings that is doubtful.
B is wrong: The groups are different people; nothing pairs one Asheville answer with one other-store answer.
C is wrong: That compares one group with a target, not two groups with each other.
Q9
What does this print?
print(stats.ttest_ind([1, 2, 3], [1, 2, np.nan], equal_var=False).pvalue)
nan
Why: SciPy's tests do not skip missing values the way pandas .mean() does. One nan makes the result nan, with no error. Fix: .dropna() first, or pass nan_policy="omit".
Q10
A Welch t-test reports a 95% confidence interval for the difference in means of -0.68 to -0.03. Without computing it, what do you know about the two-sided p-value at alpha = 0.05?
A) It is above 0.05
B) It is exactly 0.05
C) It is below 0.05
D) It cannot be known from the interval
Why: A 95% interval that excludes 0 and a two-sided p below 0.05 are the same fact seen from two sides (the lesson's test gives p = 0.030). The interval adds what p cannot: the gap could be anywhere from tiny to about two thirds of a point.
A is wrong: That would go with an interval that contains 0.
B is wrong: That would put 0 exactly on an end of the interval.
D is wrong: For the same test and confidence level, the interval and the p-value always agree on this question.
Q11
A pilot with 10 Asheville answers and 50 others gives p = 0.82 for a staff-rating gap that the full survey shows clearly (p near 3 in a billion). How should the pilot's result be worded?
A) The stores' staff ratings are the same
B) We could not detect a difference with this sample; it is too small to say much
C) The pilot proves the null hypothesis
D) The full survey must contain an error
Why: With ten answers the test has very little power, so it missed a real difference: a Type II error. A large p-value means the data are compatible with no difference, and may be just as compatible with a real one.
A is wrong: "Not significant" means "not detected", never "the same".
C is wrong: A test never proves the null; it can only fail to reject it.
D is wrong: Small samples disagreeing with large ones is exactly what low power predicts.
Q12
Five independent tests, all of true null hypotheses, each at alpha = 0.05. What does this print?
print(f"{1 - 0.95 ** 5:.0%}")
23%
Why: Each test has a 95% chance of no false alarm, so all five stay quiet with probability 0.95 to the fifth power, about 0.77. With 30 tests the chance of at least one false alarm is 79%. Count every test you ran, and correct with Holm.

Predict, Then Run: answers

Predict 4.4-1
Two correlations. Predict both before you run.
u = pd.Series([-2, -1, 0, 1, 2])
print(u.corr(u ** 2), u.corr(u * 3 + 1))
0.0 1.0
Why: u squared is completely determined by u, but the relationship is a symmetric curve with no straight-line trend, so r is 0. u * 3 + 1 is a straight rising line, so r is exactly 1, whatever the slope.
Predict 4.4-2
Pearson, then Spearman. Which one is 1?
a = pd.Series([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
b = pd.Series([1, 4, 9, 16, 100])
print(round(a.corr(b), 2), round(a.corr(b, method="spearman"), 2))
0.8 1.0
Why: b goes up every time a goes up, so the ranks match perfectly: Spearman is 1. The values do not lie on a straight line (the last jump is huge), so Pearson is lower, 0.8.
Predict 4.5-1
Two identical groups. What are t and p?
res = stats.ttest_ind([1, 2, 3], [1, 2, 3], equal_var=False)
print(res.statistic, res.pvalue)
0.0 1.0
Why: The difference in means is exactly 0, so t is 0, and every possible result is at least as extreme as this one: p = 1. Data that match the null perfectly are not surprising at all under it.
Predict 4.5-2
A table in which both rows have the same proportions. Predict the statistic, the p-value and the expected counts.
table = pd.DataFrame([[30, 10], [30, 10]])
chi = stats.chi2_contingency(table)
print(chi.statistic, chi.pvalue, chi.expected_freq.tolist())
0.0 1.0 [[30.0, 10.0], [30.0, 10.0]]
Why: The expected counts are what independence predicts, and here the real counts are exactly those, so the distance (the chi-square statistic) is 0 and p is 1.

The Lesson Pages' Own Quick Quizzes

Each lesson page ends with a five-question Quick Quiz (three options each). Learners do them at home; these are the correct options and the lesson's own reasons.

Lesson 4.4: Q1 B, Q2 C, Q3 A, Q4 B, Q5 A

Lesson 4.4 · Quiz 1
Across Pinecrest's store-days, staff on duty and revenue have r = 0.88. Staff are scheduled in advance from expected demand. What is the soundest conclusion?
B) Staffing and revenue rise and fall together, largely because both follow expected demand
Why: Expected demand drives both the schedule and the visitors who produce revenue, so it is a confounder. Among comparable days the correlation falls to about 0.14.
Lesson 4.4 · Quiz 2
For price and units sold, Pearson's r is -0.32 and Spearman's is -0.60. What does the gap suggest?
C) Units fall consistently as price rises, but along a curve, not a straight line
Why: Spearman works on ranks and detects any consistently falling pattern. Pearson only credits a straight line. A much stronger Spearman points to a monotonic relationship that is curved or distorted by extreme values.
Lesson 4.4 · Quiz 3
You compute the correlation between daily visitors and daily revenue in dollars. Then you convert revenue to cents and compute it again. What happens to r?
A) Nothing: r is unchanged
Why: r is built from z-scores, and multiplying a column by a positive constant does not change its z-scores. Correlation has no units.
Lesson 4.4 · Quiz 4
Two columns have a Pearson correlation of 0.00. Which statement is justified?
B) There is no straight-line trend; a curved relationship is still possible
Why: Pearson's r measures only straight-line association. A symmetric curve, or groups with opposite trends, can give r of zero while the variables are strongly related. Look at the scatter plot.
Lesson 4.4 · Quiz 5
A regression of revenue on visitors gives a slope of 21.19 and an intercept of -107.93. What is the best reading?
A) Within the range of the data, each additional visitor goes with about $21 more revenue on average
Why: The slope is in units of y per unit of x: about 21 dollars of revenue per additional visitor, on average, across the range of days in the data. The intercept only positions the line.

Lesson 4.5: Q1 B, Q2 A, Q3 C, Q4 A, Q5 C

Lesson 4.5 · Quiz 1
A Welch t-test comparing two stores gives p = 0.03. Which statement is correct?
B) If the stores truly had the same mean, a difference at least this large would appear in about 3% of samples
Why: A p-value describes the data under the null hypothesis: how often chance alone would give a result at least this extreme. It says nothing directly about the probability of either hypothesis.
Lesson 4.5 · Quiz 2
What does this print, and why?
q_res = stats.ttest_1samp([5, 6, 7, 8, 9], popmean=7)
print(q_res.pvalue)
A) 1.0, because the sample mean equals the target exactly, so the data are not surprising at all under the null
Why: The sample mean is exactly 7, so the t statistic is 0. Every possible sample is at least that far from the target, so the p-value is 1: the data are as compatible with the null as data can be.
Lesson 4.5 · Quiz 3
You have daily revenue for two stores on the same 700 dates and want to know whether one store earns more. Both stores are busy on the same weekends and slow in the same months. Which test fits best?
C) A paired t-test on the two stores matched by date
Why: Each date gives one value per store, so the data are pairs. Testing the daily differences removes the swings the stores share, which is what made the Bend and Flagstaff gap detectable in the lesson.
Lesson 4.5 · Quiz 4
An analyst compares 20 product categories with the company average, each at α = 0.05, and finds one category with p = 0.04. What is the most reasonable reading?
A) It could easily be a false alarm: about one such result is expected from 20 tests even if nothing differs
Why: With 20 tests at 0.05 you expect about one false alarm even if no category differs, and the chance of at least one is 64%. After a Holm or Bonferroni correction, p = 0.04 would not be significant.
Lesson 4.5 · Quiz 5
A test on 50,000 customers per group reports p = 0.001 and Cohen's d = 0.02. What should the report say?
C) The difference is very likely real and also very small; whether it matters is a practical judgment
Why: Statistical and practical significance are separate. A huge sample can detect a difference of two hundredths of a standard deviation; whether that matters is a business judgment based on the effect size and its interval.

Warm-up and Exit Ticket Answers

Warm-up
  1. A column has a skewness of 1.5. Which center and spread do you report, and what do you draw first? Answer: Median and IQR (strongly skewed, long right tail), and a histogram first: skewness does not show humps.
  2. An outlier rule flags 4% of your rows. Which two questions do you ask before removing anything? Answer: Is it a provable error? Is it part of the question I am answering? Remove only provable errors, and say what you removed.
  3. You have a frozen distribution model. Which method gives P(X at most x), and which gives P(X more than x)? Answer: model.cdf(x) and model.sf(x).
  4. In one sentence: what does the central limit theorem say becomes normal? Answer: The distribution of the sample mean (not the data): centered on the population mean, with SD sigma / sqrt(n).
  5. A 95% confidence interval for the mean order total is $165 to $209. What does the 95% describe? Answer: The method: about 95 of 100 intervals built this way contain the true mean. It is not a range for 95% of orders.
Exit ticket
  1. Staff on duty and revenue correlate at r = 0.88. Give two explanations other than "more staff cause more revenue". Answer: A confounder (expected demand, the calendar, drives both) and the reverse arrow (expected revenue decides the schedule). Selection and chance also count as explanations in general.
  2. Complete the sentence: "A p-value is the probability of ______, assuming ______." Answer: "... a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true." It is not the probability that the null is true.
  3. Besides the p-value, which two numbers belong in every test report? Answer: An effect size (how big?) and a confidence interval (how sure, and how big it could be).
