Session 12: Correlation and Hypothesis Tests

How to teach the session that turns description into claims. Before the break learners measure how two columns move together, choose between Pearson and Spearman, and take apart a correlation of 0.88 between staffing and revenue that points to exactly the wrong decision. After the break they ask whether a difference could be chance: they build a test by shuffling, read a p-value correctly, and report every test with an effect size and a confidence interval. Lesson 4.4 contains no hypothesis tests; they begin after the break.

How to Use This Guide

  • Same rhythm as every session: Frame → Live demo (you type, they type along) → Guided lab (the kit's lab files) → Start the lesson project. The lesson's exercises, quiz and journal are homework.
  • Type, do not paste. Every code block in the demo scripts is what to type, one notebook cell per step, in the course project folder. Every output shown was produced by running that code with the course environment (pandas 3.0, SciPy 1.18, statsmodels 0.15).
  • Keep the halves apart. Lesson 4.4 describes association and never tests it: if a learner asks "is 0.46 significant?" before the break, write the question on the board and answer it after. In 4.5, never let a p-value appear without an effect size and an interval, in your own speech included.
  • Words matter more than code today. Write the definition of the p-value on the board before class and point at it every time someone reads a p-value out loud.
  • The lessons are the reference. Keep statistics_correlation_analysis.html and statistics_hypothesis_testing.html open for the figures (the four scatter panels, the heatmap, the staffing diagram, the pooled and like-with-like scatters, the shuffle histogram, the power curve) and the tables (choosing Pearson or Spearman, the five explanations, what a p-value is not, the test chooser).
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. Labs for this session live in labs/module-12/: starters for learners, solutions for you.
The risk of Session 12: confident misreadings

Nothing in today's code is hard. The danger is in the sentences: "r = 0.88, so more staff sell more", "p = 0.03, so there is a 3% chance the stores are the same", "p = 0.57, so they are the same", "p = 0.003, so it is a big problem". Each one sounds right, and learners will hear all four in their working lives. Spend the session on the sentences. Two board rules carry it: every correlation gets the question "how did this data come to be?", and every p-value travels with an effect size and a confidence interval. Never phrase a p-value as the probability that a hypothesis is true, including in passing; learners copy the instructor's wording. Second risk: the session ends Module 4's teaching, and Session 13 opens with a checkpoint. Announce it clearly at the wrap-up so nobody is surprised.

Five retrieval questions (no notes, pairs, 60 seconds each)
  1. A column has a skewness of 1.5. Which center and spread do you report, and what do you draw first? Answer: Median and IQR (strongly skewed, long right tail), and a histogram first: skewness does not show humps.
  2. An outlier rule flags 4% of your rows. Which two questions do you ask before removing anything? Answer: Is it a provable error? Is it part of the question I am answering? Remove only provable errors, and say what you removed.
  3. You have a frozen distribution model. Which method gives P(X at most x), and which gives P(X more than x)? Answer: model.cdf(x) and model.sf(x).
  4. In one sentence: what does the central limit theorem say becomes normal? Answer: The distribution of the sample mean (not the data): centered on the population mean, with SD sigma / sqrt(n).
  5. A 95% confidence interval for the mean order total is $165 to $209. What does the 95% describe? Answer: The method: about 95 of 100 intervals built this way contain the true mean. It is not a range for 95% of orders.

Correlation Analysis

Timing

Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Read direction, form and strength from a scatter plot, and explain why the plot comes before the number
  • Compute Pearson and Spearman correlations with pandas and scipy, and choose between them
  • Build a correlation matrix and draw it as an annotated heatmap with Matplotlib
  • Identify a confounding variable and test for it by comparing like with like
  • Fit and interpret a simple regression line, and state what it does and does not tell you
Mental model to say

Say: "Watch two people on a dance floor. If they step forward and back together, they are positively correlated. If one steps forward whenever the other steps back, negatively. If each moves with no regard for the other, uncorrelated. Correlation measures how well their movements are synchronized. It says nothing about why: one may be leading, or both may be following the same music."

Write plot first on the board, and under it the three things to read from any scatter plot: direction, form, strength. Then the question that follows every correlation, which is the whole lesson: how did this data come to be? Tell the room up front that today's lesson has no p-values; "could this be chance?" is the second half of the session.

  2. What r is made of (3m). Say: "You already have the ingredients: z-scores. Multiply them row by row and average." Have the room predict whether the four routes agree.

  3. Spearman: correlation of ranks (3m). Say: "Price against units sold. Obviously related, and it scores a feeble -0.32. It is just not straight."

print("Spearman        :", round(price.corr(units, method="spearman"), 3))
print("Pearson on ranks:", round(price.rank().corr(units.rank()), 3))

    Pearson         : -0.319
Spearman        : -0.6

Pearson of logs : -0.628
    Spearman is Pearson on the ranks: it measures whether one goes consistently up as the other goes down, straight or not. When the two disagree, there is a curve or an influential point. Spearman's other home is ordinal data: overall satisfaction and the recommend score give 0.63 in the lesson. Habit for the board: compute both.

  8. Tests 2 and 3 (3m). Say: "Two more angles. Hold visitors constant. And ask whether each visitor spends more on well-staffed days." 
    def residuals(target, predictor):
    fit = stats.linregress(predictor, target)
    return target - (fit.intercept + fit.slope * predictor)

staff_left = residuals(staff, sales["visitors"])
revenue_left = residuals(revenue, sales["visitors"])
print("partial r, controlling for visitors:",
      round(staff_left.corr(revenue_left), 3))
print("r, staff and spend per visitor     :",
      round(staff.corr(revenue / sales["visitors"]), 3))
    partial r, controlling for visitors: 0.068
r, staff and spend per visitor     : 0.032
    Put the verdict table on the board: 0.88 pooled, 0.14 like with like, 0.07 holding visitors constant, 0.03 with spend per visitor. Three checks agree. Then the honest limit: this does not prove staffing has no effect (no day in the data is badly understaffed). Observational data can rule a simple story out; to rule one in you need an experiment, which is Lesson 4.6.
  9. Correlation is not causation, in a list (1m). Write the lesson's five explanations for any correlation on the board without code: A causes B; B causes A; a confounder causes both; selection; chance. Ask which ones apply to staffing. Reverse arrow (expected revenue causes the schedule) and confounder (the calendar). Chance is ruled out by 2,897 days; selection by how the file was built.
  10. A first regression line (3m). Say: "Correlation tells you how tightly points follow a line. Regression tells you which line." 
    fit = stats.linregress(sales["visitors"], sales["revenue"])

intercept: -107.93

    Read every number in units: about $21 more revenue per extra visitor, on average; the intercept only positions the line (no day is near zero visitors); visitors account for 89% of the variance. Three cautions: do not extrapolate beyond 125 to 1,164 visitors; the line from revenue to visitors is a different line; a slope is not a causal effect (the $1,154 per staff member was a correct slope and almost entirely confound).
  11. Two mistakes on purpose (2m). Say: "Two that give a clean-looking answer." 
    print(np.corrcoef(daily["visitors"], daily["revenue"])[0, 1])

0.0
    First: six missing revenues and NumPy returns nan; pandas drops missing pairs. Second: a perfect curve scores 0. r near zero means no linear relationship; only the scatter plot can say there is none.
Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Reading r as a slope or a percentage. "r = 0.94, so 94 cents a visitor" or "r = 0.5, half related". Coach: "r measures how tightly the points hug a line, not how steep it is. For the slope, fit the line. For 'how much does it account for', square r." Have them print r ** 2 for 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9.
  • Reading a correlation as a cause. The staffing proposal is the example everyone will make in real life. Coach with the five explanations on the board and one question: "Was either variable decided by someone using information related to the other?" Then: "Does it survive comparing like with like?"
  • Trusting r without the picture. r = 0 for a curve, nan from np.corrcoef with a missing value, or a ValueError from daily.corr() on a table with text. Coach: "Show me the scatter plot." For the errors: use pandas .corr() (which drops missing pairs and says nothing, so print the n), and select numeric columns or pass numeric_only=True.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab (cold call, then pair-share):

  1. "Pearson says -0.32 and Spearman says -0.60 for the same two columns. What do you suspect, and what do you look at?" Answer: A consistent relationship that is curved (or an influential point). Look at the scatter plot, perhaps on log axes.
  2. "r = 0.88 between staff and revenue. Name two explanations other than 'staff cause revenue'." Answer: A confounder (expected demand, the calendar, drives both) and the reverse arrow (expected revenue decides the schedule).
  3. "The line says $21 of revenue per extra visitor and the intercept is -$108. Does a store with no visitors lose $108?" Answer: No. No day is anywhere near zero visitors; the intercept only positions the line. Do not interpret it, and do not extrapolate.

Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Explain the logic of a hypothesis test in plain words, and say what a p-value is and what it is not

  • Choose and run a one-sample, Welch two-sample or paired t-test, a chi-square test, or a rank-based test for the data in front of you

Mental model to say

Say: "A court starts from 'innocent until proven guilty'. It does not ask the defendant to prove innocence. It asks whether the evidence would be surprising if the defendant were innocent. If it would be very surprising, the court rejects innocence. If not, the verdict is 'not guilty', which is not the same as 'proven innocent'. A hypothesis test works the same way. The starting assumption, the null hypothesis, is 'nothing is going on: any difference is chance'. Then you ask how surprising your data would be if that were true."

Write the two verdicts on the board: reject the null and fail to reject the null (never "accept"). Then say why this lesson matters more than any other in the module: "The p-value is the most misread number in statistics. By the end of today you will read it correctly, and you will never report one alone."

Live demo (you type, they follow)

New notebook session12_tests.ipynb. The numbers are the lesson's own; the two simulations use default_rng(42) created inside their cells, so they reproduce on every machine. Step 4 is said, not typed. If time runs short, say step 7 in one sentence and show step 10's numbers from the lesson page; never cut steps 2 to 6 (the test built by hand, the p-value, Welch with its interval and effect size).

  1. The question (3m). New notebook. Say: "Head office noticed that Asheville shoppers give a lower recommend score. Before anyone schedules a meeting about Asheville: could that gap be luck?" 
    import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

survey = pd.read_csv("data/survey.csv")
in_store = survey[survey["store"] != "Online"]
is_ash = in_store["store"] == "Asheville"
asheville = in_store.loc[is_ash, "recommend"].dropna()
others = in_store.loc[~is_ash, "recommend"].dropna()

for name, g in [("Asheville", asheville), ("Other stores", others)]:
    print(f"{name:<12} n = {len(g)}  mean = {g.mean():.2f}"
          f"  sd = {g.std():.2f}")
observed = asheville.mean() - others.mean()
print(f"Observed difference: {observed:.3f}")
    Asheville    n = 197  mean = 6.98  sd = 2.05
Other stores n = 752  mean = 7.34  sd = 2.04
Observed difference: -0.357
    About a third of a point on an eleven-point scale, with answers spread about 2 points either side. Is 0.36 a lot? Looking will not tell you. You need to know how big a gap chance alone produces with groups of this size.
  2. Build chance yourself: shuffle the labels (4m). Say: "If the store makes no difference, the 'Asheville' label is a sticker that could have landed on anyone. Peel off all 949 stickers, shuffle, stick them back, measure the gap. Ten thousand times." 
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
pooled = np.concatenate([asheville, others])
n_ash = len(asheville)
shuffled_diffs = np.empty(10_000)
for i in range(10_000):
    shuffled = rng.permutation(pooled)
    shuffled_diffs[i] = shuffled[:n_ash].mean() - shuffled[n_ash:].mean()

print(shuffled_diffs[:5].round(3))
print(f"typical chance gap (SD): {shuffled_diffs.std():.3f}")
    [-0.03   0.034 -0.216  0.277  0.124]
typical chance gap (SD): 0.165
    Each number is a gap between two groups that cannot truly differ. Their spread, about 0.165, is a standard error measured by simulation: Session 11's idea, for a difference. The real gap is a little more than two of those from zero. Show the lesson's histogram with the real gap marked in the tail.
  3. Count: the p-value (3m). Say: "How often did chance produce a gap at least this large, in either direction?" 
    as_extreme = np.abs(shuffled_diffs) >= abs(observed)
print(as_extreme.sum(), "of 10,000 shuffles gave a gap at least this large")
print(f"p-value = {as_extreme.mean():.3f}")
    300 of 10,000 shuffles gave a gap at least this large
p-value = 0.030
    Write the definition on the board and leave it there all session: the p-value is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. Then the four steps of every test: state what "nothing going on" means; work out what that produces; see where the real result falls; report how rare it is. Vocabulary: H0, H1, alpha = 0.05 chosen before looking, "reject" or "fail to reject" (never "accept").
  4. What a p-value is not (2m). Read the lesson's table aloud, no code. Have learners say "no" out loud to each. "There is a 3% chance the null is true": no, p is computed assuming the null. "97% chance Asheville is worse": no, same mistake turned around. "The gap is large": no, p mixes effect size with sample size. "p = 0.20 means the stores are the same": no, not detected is not absent. And 0.049 against 0.051 is the same evidence: report the number.
  5. The shortcut: Welch's t-test (3m). Say: "For a difference in means there is a classic shortcut: the difference divided by its standard error, read off the t distribution. Welch's version does not assume equal variances. SciPy's default does, so type equal_var=False every time." 
    res = stats.ttest_ind(asheville, others, equal_var=False)
ci = res.confidence_interval(confidence_level=0.95)
print(f"t = {res.statistic:.2f}, df = {res.df:.0f}, p = {res.pvalue:.3f}")
print(f"95% CI for the difference: {ci.low:.2f} to {ci.high:.2f}")
    t = -2.17, df = 305, p = 0.030
95% CI for the difference: -0.68 to -0.03
    The same p as the shuffle: one by simulation, one by theory. The interval is the more useful half: Asheville is between 0.68 and 0.03 points lower. It excludes 0, which is the same fact as p below 0.05, and it shows what p cannot: the true gap might be meaningful or almost nothing.
  6. How big? Cohen's d (2m). Say: "A p-value never travels alone. It comes with an effect size and an interval." 
    def cohens_d(x, y):
    nx, ny = len(x), len(y)
    pooled = (((nx - 1) * np.var(x, ddof=1) + (ny - 1) * np.var(y, ddof=1))
              / (nx + ny - 2))
    return (np.mean(x) - np.mean(y)) / np.sqrt(pooled)

print(f"Cohen's d = {cohens_d(asheville, others):.2f}")
    Cohen's d = -0.17
    Near 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large: this is small. Say the honest sentence: "Asheville shoppers rated Pinecrest 0.36 points lower on average (95% CI 0.03 to 0.68 lower; Welch p = 0.030; d = -0.17, small)." Evidence worth following up, nowhere near a crisis.
  7. Significant is not the same as important (2m). Say: "Now the opposite case. Target score 7.5, all 1,746 answers." 
    everyone = survey["recommend"].dropna()
res1 = stats.ttest_1samp(everyone, popmean=7.5)
ci1 = res1.confidence_interval()
print(f"n = {len(everyone)}, mean = {everyone.mean():.2f}")
print(f"p = {res1.pvalue:.4f}")
print(f"95% CI {ci1.low:.2f} to {ci1.high:.2f}, "
      f"d = {(everyone.mean() - 7.5) / everyone.std():.2f}")
    n = 1746, mean = 7.36
p = 0.0028
95% CI 7.27 to 7.45, d = -0.07
    A smaller p than Asheville's and a tiny shortfall (d = -0.07). With 1,746 answers even a small miss is detectable. Write on the board: statistically significant = unlikely to be chance alone; practically significant = big enough to matter. The first is a calculation, the second a judgment.
  8. Pairs: the same days, two stores (3m). Say: "Bend against Flagstaff, visitors, on the same dates. Watch the verdict change with the test." 
    daily = pd.read_csv("data/daily_sales.csv", parse_dates=["date"])
visitors = daily.pivot(index="date", columns="store", values="visitors")
pair = visitors[["Bend", "Flagstaff"]].dropna()
wrong = stats.ttest_ind(pair["Bend"], pair["Flagstaff"], equal_var=False)
right = stats.ttest_rel(pair["Bend"], pair["Flagstaff"])
print(len(pair), "days; mean difference",
      round((pair["Bend"] - pair["Flagstaff"]).mean(), 1))
print(f"as independent groups: p = {wrong.pvalue:.3f}")
print(f"as pairs             : p = {right.pvalue:.4f}")
    722 days; mean difference 6.5
as independent groups: p = 0.274
as pairs             : p = 0.0024
    Traffic swings from a February Tuesday to an August Saturday in both stores together. The independent test sees all that swing as noise; pairing by date subtracts it out. Real and small: about 6.5 visitors a day. Caveat to say once: consecutive days are not fully independent, so daily-data p-values are approximate.
  9. Categories: chi-square (3m). Say: "Means are for numbers. When both variables are categories, compare counts." 
    orders = pd.read_csv("data/orders.csv")
orders["returned"] = orders["status"] == "returned"
table = pd.crosstab(orders["channel"], orders["returned"])
chi = stats.chi2_contingency(table)
print(table)
print(f"chi-square = {chi.statistic:.1f}, df = {chi.dof}")
print(f"p = {chi.pvalue:.1e}")
print("smallest expected count:", round(chi.expected_freq.min(), 1))
rates = orders.groupby("channel")["returned"].mean()
print(f"online {rates['online']:.1%}, store {rates['store']:.1%}")
    returned  False  True 
channel               
online    11964    890
store      7195    253
chi-square = 109.7, df = 1
p = 1.1e-25
smallest expected count: 419.3
online 6.9%, store 3.4%
    The null: channel and returning are unrelated. The expected counts are the assumption check (about 5 or more in every cell; the smallest is 419). The effect: online orders come back twice as often (6.9% against 3.4%). Cramér's V is only 0.07 here, so for a 2 x 2 table report the rates and their ratio: that is what a reader can act on.
  10. Ratings: Mann-Whitney (3m). Say: "A t-test compares means, and a mean assumes equal steps between 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. For ratings, use ranks." 
    staff_ash = in_store.loc[is_ash, "sat_staff"].dropna()
staff_oth = in_store.loc[~is_ash, "sat_staff"].dropna()
mw = stats.mannwhitneyu(staff_ash, staff_oth)
print(f"n = {len(staff_ash)} and {len(staff_oth)}, p = {mw.pvalue:.1e}")
print("probability of superiority:",
      round(mw.statistic / (len(staff_ash) * len(staff_oth)), 2))
    n = 193 and 754, p = 3.0e-09
probability of superiority: 0.37
    This is the gap learners described in Session 11 (57.5% satisfied against 75 to 79%). Now it has a test: p about 3 in a billion. The effect size: pick one Asheville shopper and one other-store shopper at random; the Asheville rating is the higher one 37% of the time (50% would be no difference).
  11. False alarms, watched (3m). Say: "A test can be wrong two ways. Here is the first one, happening." Draw two groups from the same pool, so the null is true by construction. 
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
pool = in_store["recommend"].dropna().to_numpy()
p_null = np.array([
    stats.ttest_ind(rng.choice(pool, 197), rng.choice(pool, 752),
                    equal_var=False).pvalue
    for _ in range(2_000)])
share = (p_null < 0.05).mean()
print(f"{share:.1%} of 2,000 tests of a true null were significant")
    5.3% of 2,000 tests of a true null were significant
    About 5%, as alpha promises: one test in twenty rings the alarm when nothing is going on (a Type I error). The other way to be wrong is a miss (Type II); the chance of catching a real effect is power, and it depends on sample size: with 50 per group a small real effect (d = 0.2) is caught only 17% of the time. Then multiple comparisons in one line: 30 tests of true nulls give a 79% chance of at least one false alarm. The lesson's scan of 6 age groups x 5 items finds 6 "significant" results, 3 after Holm's correction. Count every test you ran.
  12. A mistake on purpose (1m). Say: "SciPy does not skip missing values." 
    raw = in_store.loc[in_store["store"] == "Asheville", "recommend"]
print(stats.ttest_ind(raw, others, equal_var=False).pvalue)
print(round(stats.ttest_ind(raw, others, equal_var=False,
                            nan_policy="omit").pvalue, 3))
    nan
0.03
    nan with no error. Fix: .dropna() first, or nan_policy="omit". Close with the reporting template from the lesson, on the board: what was compared (and n) · the estimate · its 95% CI · the test and p · the effect size in words · caveats (how the data were collected, how many tests).
Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Reading the p-value as the probability that the null is true ("a 3% chance the stores are the same", "97% sure Asheville is worse"). Coach with one question every time: "What was assumed when p was computed?" Then have them rewrite the sentence starting with "If there were no difference, ...". The board definition stays up all session.
  • "Not significant" read as "no difference", and "significant" read as "big". Coach both with the effect size and the interval: "What does the confidence interval allow?" For a small sample show the lesson's pilot (10 answers, p = 0.82 for a real gap). For a huge sample show the target test (p = 0.003, d = -0.07). Two separate questions: is it chance? does it matter?
  • Silent mechanical errors. nan from a missing value (SciPy does not skip: .dropna() or nan_policy="omit"); stats.ttest_ind(x, y) without equal_var=False (Student's test, no warning); chi2_contingency on raw columns instead of a crosstab table; a paired test on two columns dropped separately (misaligned days); alternative="less" chosen after looking. Coach: "Read your call out loud and say what each argument means."

Checks for understanding
