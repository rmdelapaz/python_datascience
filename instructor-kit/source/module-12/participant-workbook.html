4.4

Correlation Analysis

95 min alone
By the end you can
  • Read direction, form and strength from a scatter plot, and explain why the plot comes before the number
  • Compute Pearson and Spearman correlations with pandas and scipy, and choose between them
  • Build a correlation matrix and draw it as an annotated heatmap with Matplotlib
  • Identify a confounding variable and test for it by comparing like with like
  • Fit and interpret a simple regression line, and state what it does and does not tell you
Key ideas
  • Plot first. A scatter plot shows direction (rising or falling), form (straight, curved, clusters) and strength (how tightly points hug the form). r summarizes one kind of pattern only.
  • Pearson's r measures straight-line association from -1 to +1. It is the average product of z-scores: no units, symmetric, and sensitive to extreme points. x.corr(y).
  • r is not a slope and not a percentage. The share of variance a straight line accounts for is r squared: r = 0.5 is 25%.
  • r = 0 means no linear trend, not no relationship. A perfect curve can score 0.
  • Spearman's rho is Pearson on ranks (method="spearman"): consistent up or down, straight or not. Use it for curves, outliers and ordinal data. A gap between the two means a curve or an influential point. Compute both.
  • A correlation matrix (df[cols].corr()) shows every pair. A block of high correlations usually has one common driver. Heatmaps: a diverging colormap with the scale fixed at -1 to 1.
  • Correlation is not causation. A causes B, B causes A, a confounder causes both, selection, or chance. Ask how the data came to be.
  • A confounder drives both variables. Test for it by comparing like with like: subtract each group's mean (groupby().transform("mean")) and correlate the deviations, or use a partial correlation.
  • The staffing verdict: staff and revenue r = 0.88 pooled, 0.14 among comparable days, 0.07 holding visitors constant. The calendar drives both.
  • A regression line (stats.linregress(x, y), predictor first) gives the slope in real units. Predict only within the data's range. A slope describes; it is not the effect of changing x.
Worked example

Do orders with more product lines cost more? Measure two ways, then fit a line to get the answer in dollars. (No test here: whether a pattern could be chance is Lesson 4.5's question.)

import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

orders = pd.read_csv("data/orders.csv")
items = pd.read_csv("data/order_items.csv")
lines = items.groupby("order_id").size().rename("lines")
both = orders.merge(lines, on="order_id")

print("orders:", len(both))
print("Pearson :", round(both["lines"].corr(both["order_total"]), 3))
print("Spearman:", round(both["lines"].corr(both["order_total"],
                                            method="spearman"), 3))
fit = stats.linregress(both["lines"], both["order_total"])
print("slope   :", round(fit.slope, 2), "dollars per extra product line")
print("r squared:", round(fit.rvalue ** 2, 3))
orders: 20302
Pearson : 0.54
Spearman: 0.646
slope   : 86.46 dollars per extra product line
r squared: 0.291

Spearman (0.65) is clearly above Pearson (0.54): the relationship is consistent but not a straight band, because a few orders with one bulk line are very large. Each extra product line goes with about $86 more, on average, and the line accounts for about 30% of the variance: the number of lines is far from the whole story (what is on them matters more). Notice the reading: "goes with", not "adds".

4.5

Hypothesis Testing

110 min alone
By the end you can
  • Explain the logic of a hypothesis test in plain words, and say what a p-value is and what it is not
  • Build a permutation test by simulation and match it to SciPy's ready-made tests
  • Choose and run a one-sample, Welch two-sample or paired t-test, a chi-square test, or a rank-based test for the data in front of you
  • Report every test with an effect size and a confidence interval, and check its assumptions on the data
  • Describe Type I and Type II errors and power, and correct for multiple comparisons
Key ideas
  • A hypothesis test asks one question: how surprising would these data be if nothing were going on?
  • The four steps: state what "nothing going on" means (the null, H0); work out what that would produce; see where the real result falls; report how rare it is.
  • The p-value is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability that the null is true, and it is not a measure of size.
  • Verdicts: p below alpha (usually 0.05, chosen before looking): reject the null. Otherwise: fail to reject. Never "accept". 0.049 and 0.051 are the same evidence: report the number.
  • A permutation test builds "nothing going on" by shuffling the labels many times. The named tests reach the same answer with theory.
  • Choosing: two independent groups: Welch (ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var=False)). One group against a target: ttest_1samp. The same units twice: paired (ttest_rel). Counts in categories: chi-square (chi2_contingency(crosstab)). Ratings or ranks: Mann-Whitney (mannwhitneyu).
  • Every p-value travels with an effect size and a confidence interval. Cohen's d (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large); for tables, the rates and their ratio.
  • Statistically significant = unlikely to be chance alone. Practically significant = big enough to matter. Large samples make tiny effects significant.
  • Two ways to be wrong: a false alarm (Type I, rate alpha) and a miss (Type II). Power is the chance of catching a real effect; it rises with sample size. "Not significant" means "not detected".
  • Many tests, many false alarms. Count every test you ran, correct with Holm (multipletests(..., method="holm")), and label scanned results as exploratory.
  • Every test assumes independent observations. SciPy does not skip missing values: .dropna() first.
  • Report template: what was compared (and n) · the estimate · its 95% CI · the test and p · the effect size in words · caveats.
Worked example

Do Bend and Flagstaff shoppers differ in how likely they are to recommend Pinecrest? Hypotheses first, then the test, the interval and a careful sentence.

import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

survey = pd.read_csv("data/survey.csv")
bend = survey.loc[survey["store"] == "Bend", "recommend"].dropna()
flag = survey.loc[survey["store"] == "Flagstaff", "recommend"].dropna()
# H0: Bend and Flagstaff shoppers have the same mean recommend score.
# H1: the means differ (two-sided). alpha = 0.05. Welch t-test.

res = stats.ttest_ind(bend, flag, equal_var=False)
ci = res.confidence_interval()
print(f"Bend n = {len(bend)}, mean {bend.mean():.2f}; "
      f"Flagstaff n = {len(flag)}, mean {flag.mean():.2f}")
print(f"difference {bend.mean() - flag.mean():.2f}, "
      f"95% CI {ci.low:.2f} to {ci.high:.2f}")
print(f"t = {res.statistic:.2f}, p = {res.pvalue:.2f}")
Bend n = 223, mean 7.27; Flagstaff n = 207, mean 7.39
difference -0.11, 95% CI -0.51 to 0.28
t = -0.57, p = 0.57

p = 0.57: a gap this size (about a tenth of a point) turns up by chance more than half the time, so you fail to reject the null. The careful sentence: "We did not detect a difference between Bend and Flagstaff shoppers; the data are consistent with Bend being anywhere from about half a point lower to about a quarter of a point higher." It does not say the two stores are the same.

Glossary

  • Scatter plot: one point per row, one variable on each axis; read direction, form and strength from it.
  • Pearson's r: a number from -1 to +1 for straight-line association: the average product of z-scores.
  • Spearman's rho: Pearson's r on the ranks: consistent (monotonic) association, robust to outliers, suited to ordinal data.
  • Monotonic: consistently rising or consistently falling, whether or not along a straight line.
  • Correlation matrix: a square table of the correlation of every pair of columns.
  • Confounder: a third variable that influences both variables being correlated.
  • Partial correlation: the correlation of two variables after removing the straight-line effect of a third from each.
  • Simpson's paradox: a pooled correlation with the opposite sign to the correlation inside every group.
  • Regression line: the least-squares line y = intercept + slope x; predictor first in stats.linregress(x, y).
  • r squared: the share of the variance in y that the straight line accounts for.
  • Residual: actual value minus the value the line predicts.
  • Null hypothesis (H0): the "nothing is going on" claim assumed in order to test it.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1): what you conclude if the null does not hold up; usually two-sided.
  • p-value: the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed.
  • Significance level (alpha): the cutoff chosen before looking, usually 0.05.
  • Permutation test: a test that builds the null by shuffling group labels many times.
  • Welch's t-test: a two-sample t-test that does not assume equal variances (equal_var=False).
  • Paired t-test: a test on the differences within matched pairs (ttest_rel).
  • Chi-square test: a test of independence for a table of counts (chi2_contingency).
  • Mann-Whitney U: a rank-based test for two independent groups; effect size U / (n1 x n2).
  • Effect size: how big a difference is: Cohen's d, a rate ratio, Cramér's V, a probability of superiority.
  • Type I / Type II error: a false alarm (rate alpha) / a miss.
  • Power: the probability that a test detects an effect that truly exists; it grows with sample size.
  • Multiple comparisons: running many tests, which multiplies false alarms; correct with Holm.
