4.2

Descriptive Statistics II: Shape, Position and Outliers

90 min alone
By the end you can
  • Describe the shape of a distribution with a histogram and a skewness value, and say what the shape implies for your choice of summary
  • Locate a value within its column using percentiles, quantiles and z-scores
  • Apply the IQR and z-score outlier rules, explain why they disagree, and choose a defensible action for flagged values
  • Read every line of describe() and every part of a box plot
  • Summarize by group with groupby().agg(), and summarize 1-to-5 survey answers as the ordered categories they are
Key ideas
  • Four things to know about a column: center, spread, shape, and one value's position in it. Shape decides which summaries are fair.
  • Skewness (.skew()): 0 for a symmetric pile, positive for a long right tail. Between -0.5 and 0.5: mean and SD are fair. Beyond 1: median and IQR. It is not robust and does not show humps: always look at a histogram.
  • A logarithm tames a right tail. Order totals: skew 5.48; np.log(totals): -0.41.
  • Percentiles and quantiles locate by rank. totals.quantile(0.90) takes a fraction (0 to 1). np.percentile(totals, 90) takes 0 to 100. The reverse question: (totals <= 500).mean().
  • A z-score locates by distance: z = (value - mean) / SD. It makes different scales comparable. Standardizing gives mean 0 and SD 1 and does not change the shape.
  • Two outlier rules. IQR fences: below Q1 - 1.5 x IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 x IQR. z-scores: beyond 3. They disagree, because the mean and SD are inflated by the values being judged.
  • A flag is a question, not a verdict. Provable error? Fix or remove it, and note what you did. Real and part of your question? Keep it and use the median and IQR. Real but a different kind of thing? Analyze separately and say so.
  • describe(): count (non-missing), mean, std (sample SD), min, 25%, 50%, 75%, max. Compare mean with 50%, and the two halves of the box.
  • A box plot: the line is the median; the box is Q1 to Q3; each whisker ends at the last data point inside the fence; separate points are the flagged values. Good for comparing groups; hides humps.
  • Grouped summaries always carry their n. count is the number of values, size the number of rows.
  • Survey answers on a 1-to-5 scale are ordinal: ordered categories. Lead with the distribution, the median and the share satisfied (4 or 5), and check who did not answer.
Worked example

Was Black Friday 2025 an unusual day for foot traffic at the Flagstaff store? Locate it two ways: by distance (a z-score) and by rank (a percentile).

import pandas as pd

daily = pd.read_csv("data/daily_sales.csv", parse_dates=["date"])
flagstaff = daily.loc[daily["store"] == "Flagstaff"].set_index("date")
visitors = flagstaff["visitors"]

day = visitors.loc["2025-11-28"]                  # Black Friday 2025
z = (day - visitors.mean()) / visitors.std()
rank = (visitors <= day).mean()

print("visitors that day:", day)
print("store mean:", round(visitors.mean(), 1))
print("store SD  :", round(visitors.std(), 1))
print("z-score:", round(z, 2))
print(f"at or below this on {rank:.1%} of Flagstaff's days")

boise = daily.loc[daily["store"] == "Boise", "visitors"]
print("the same count in Boise: z =",
      round((day - boise.mean()) / boise.std(), 2))
visitors that day: 596
store mean: 328.5
store SD  : 117.4
z-score: 2.28
at or below this on 96.8% of Flagstaff's days
the same count in Boise: z = 0.68

596 visitors is 2.28 standard deviations above Flagstaff's average, and only about 3 days in 100 were busier. The same 596 visitors in Boise would be 0.68 SDs above average: a good day, nothing more. A z-score is always measured against a particular group's mean and SD, so say which group.

4.3

Probability Distributions

100 min alone
By the end you can
  • Explain what a random variable and a probability distribution are, and tell discrete from continuous
  • Choose between the uniform, normal, binomial and Poisson distributions for a situation, and check the choice against data
  • Answer probability questions with scipy.stats using pmf/pdf, cdf, sf and ppf
  • Simulate random outcomes reproducibly with np.random.default_rng and use simulation to check a calculation
  • Demonstrate the central limit theorem by simulation, compute a standard error, and build and correctly interpret a 95% confidence interval for a mean
Key ideas
  • A random variable is a number decided by chance. Its distribution lists the possible values with their probabilities: the vending machine's stocking list.
  • Discrete variables are counted (returns in a day). Continuous ones are measured (spend per visitor), and only ranges have probability.
  • Four workhorses. Uniform: equally likely anywhere in a range. Normal: sums and averages of many small effects (mean, SD). Binomial: successes in n independent tries with the same p. Poisson: events in an interval at a steady rate (mean about equal to variance).
  • Freeze, then ask. model = stats.norm(loc=mean, scale=sd). cdf(x): this or less. sf(x): more than this. ppf(q): which value has q below it. pmf(k): exactly k (discrete). rvs(size, random_state=rng): random draws.
  • scale is the SD, not the variance. For stats.uniform, loc is the start and scale is the width.
  • For a count, "5 or more" is sf(4).
  • Simulation checks any formula. Create one seeded generator at the top (rng = np.random.default_rng(42)) and keep drawing from it. Never create it inside a loop.
  • 68-95-99.7: in a normal distribution about 68% of values are within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2, 99.7% within 3. It is a property of the normal shape only.
  • A model's assumptions are part of it. Check the fit against data before trusting its answers.
  • Central limit theorem: means of random samples are approximately normal, centered on the population mean, with SD sigma / sqrt(n), whatever the population's shape. The data itself does not become normal.
  • Standard error: SE = s / sqrt(n): how much a sample mean varies from sample to sample. The SD describes single values and does not shrink with more data.
  • 95% confidence interval: mean plus or minus about 2 SE (stats.t.interval). The 95% describes the method: about 95 of 100 intervals built this way contain the true mean. It is not a range for individual values.
Worked example

About 7% of Pinecrest's orders are paid with a gift card. Out of the next 30 orders, how many will be? A fixed number of tries (30), each with the same probability: a binomial model. Compute, then check by simulation.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
orders = pd.read_csv("data/orders.csv")
p_gift = (orders["payment_method"] == "gift_card").mean()

batch = stats.binom(n=30, p=p_gift)               # gift-card orders out of 30
print("share paid by gift card:", round(p_gift, 4))
print("expected in a batch of 30:", round(batch.mean(), 2))
print(f"P(none)      = {batch.pmf(0):.3f}")
print(f"P(5 or more) = {batch.sf(4):.3f}")

sim = rng.binomial(n=30, p=p_gift, size=100_000)  # check by counting
print("simulated P(5 or more):", round((sim >= 5).mean(), 3))
share paid by gift card: 0.074
expected in a batch of 30: 2.22
P(none)      = 0.100
P(5 or more) = 0.067
simulated P(5 or more): 0.067

About 2 gift-card orders per batch on average; one batch in ten has none; five or more happens in about 7% of batches. "5 or more" is sf(4): more than 4. The simulation, which only counts, agrees with the formula to the third decimal. The model assumes each order is independent with the same probability, which is worth doubting in December.

Glossary

  • Distribution: how the values of a column (or the outcomes of a random variable) are spread: which values occur and how often.
  • Skewness: a number for lopsidedness: 0 for symmetric, positive for a long right tail, negative for a long left tail.
  • Percentile / quantile: the value with a given share of the data at or below it, on a 0-to-100 scale (percentile) or a 0-to-1 scale (quantile).
  • z-score: (value - mean) / SD: how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.
  • Outlier: a value that sits far from the rest of its column. A candidate for a closer look, not automatically an error.
  • IQR fences: Q1 - 1.5 x IQR and Q3 + 1.5 x IQR. Values beyond them are flagged; box plots use this rule.
  • Box plot: a drawing of the median, the quartiles, whiskers to the last points inside the fences, and the flagged points.
  • Ordinal data: ordered categories with no guaranteed distances between them, such as 1-to-5 survey answers (a Likert item).
  • Top-two-box: the share of answers that are 4 or 5 on a 1-to-5 scale.
  • Missing not at random: missing for a reason connected to what is being measured, so the answers you have may not represent the group.
  • Random variable: a number whose value is decided by chance.
  • Discrete / continuous: counted in separate values / measured on a continuous range.
  • Expected value: the long-run average of a random variable.
  • Frozen distribution: a scipy.stats distribution with its parameters fixed, ready to answer questions.
  • pmf / pdf: the probability of exactly k (discrete) / the height of the curve at x (continuous; not a probability).
  • cdf / sf / ppf: the probability of x or less / of more than x / the value with probability q below it.
  • Normal distribution: the bell curve, described by its mean and SD.
  • Binomial distribution: the number of successes in n independent tries with the same probability p.
  • Poisson distribution: the number of events in a fixed interval at a steady average rate; its mean equals its variance.
  • Overdispersion: more day-to-day variation than a single-rate Poisson allows: variance well above the mean.
  • Central limit theorem (CLT): means of random samples are approximately normal, centered on the population mean, with SD sigma / sqrt(n).
  • Standard error (SE): s / sqrt(n): how much a sample mean varies from sample to sample.
  • Confidence interval: an estimate with a margin of error, from a method that captures the true value a stated share of the time (95%).
