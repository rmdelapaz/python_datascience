4.2 · Descriptive Statistics II: Shape, Position and Outliers: self-check

Q1
Pinecrest's order totals have a skewness of 5.48. After np.log(totals) the skewness is -0.41. What does that tell you?
A) The logarithm deleted the twelve bulk orders
B) The original totals contained errors that the logarithm repaired
C) On the log scale the pile is roughly symmetric, so methods that prefer symmetric data will behave better there
D) The mean of the logs is the log of the mean
Why: A logarithm turns equal ratios into equal distances, which pulls in a long right tail. Between -0.5 and 0.5 counts as roughly symmetric. No row was removed or changed.
A is wrong: Every one of the 20,302 orders is still there; a transform changes the scale, not the rows.
B is wrong: The totals are correct. Skew is a shape, not an error.
D is wrong: That is false in general, and it is not what skewness measures.
Q2
What does this print?
print(pd.Series([10, 20, 30, 40]).quantile(0.5))
25.0
Why: The 0.5 quantile is the median. With four values the cut falls between 20 and 30, and pandas interpolates halfway by default. quantile takes a fraction from 0 to 1; quantile(50) raises a ValueError.
Q3
A $12,000 day has a z-score of 3.05 at the Asheville store and 0.50 at the Boise store. What does that mean?
A) The day is far above Asheville's own average, measured in Asheville's SDs, and only a little above Boise's
B) Asheville took 3.05 times as much revenue as Boise that day
C) The day was in the top 3.05% of Asheville's days
D) z-scores from different stores cannot be compared
Why: z = (value - mean) / SD, using each store's own mean and SD. $12,000 is about three SDs above Asheville's mean of $5,704 and half an SD above Boise's mean of $10,144: exceptional in one store, routine in the other.
B is wrong: A z-score is a distance in SDs, not a ratio of revenues. The revenue is $12,000 in both cases.
C is wrong: A z-score is not a percentage. Turning it into "how rare" needs the shape of the distribution (in the data, 1.5% of Asheville's days reach $12,000).
D is wrong: Comparing across different scales is exactly what z-scores are for: the units cancel.
Q4
On the order totals the IQR rule flags 848 orders and the rule "|z| above 3" flags 217. Why does the z-score rule flag so many fewer?
A) The z-score rule only looks at the smallest values
B) The IQR rule counts duplicated orders twice
C) z-scores cannot be computed for skewed data
D) The mean and SD are inflated by the same large orders being judged, which pushes the z-score line outward; the quartiles are not
Why: The z rule is built on the mean and SD, which are not robust: the tail stretches them, so the cutoff lands near $732. The IQR fences are built on quartiles, which the tail cannot move, so the fence stays at $544.
A is wrong: Both rules look at both sides; here neither can flag a small order (the lower fence is negative).
B is wrong: There are no duplicates in orders.csv, and the rule does not count rows twice.
C is wrong: A z-score is always a valid distance. What fails on skewed data is reading it as "how rare".
Q5
What does this print?
s = pd.Series([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 50])
q1, q3 = s.quantile(0.25), s.quantile(0.75)
upper = q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1)
print(upper, (s > upper).sum())
14.5 1
Why: Q1 is 3.25 and Q3 is 7.75, so the IQR is 4.5 and the upper fence is 7.75 + 6.75 = 14.5. Only the 50 lies beyond it. A box plot of this Series would end its upper whisker at 9, the largest value inside the fence, and draw the 50 as a separate point.
Q6
In the survey, Online shoppers' mean sat_staff is 4.07, computed from 248 of the 824 Online respondents. What is the honest way to report it?
A) Report 4.07 as the staff satisfaction of Online shoppers
B) Report it with its n and say who answered: 70% of Online shoppers skipped the question, so it describes the minority who dealt with staff
C) Fill the 576 missing answers with 4.07 so that the n is 824
D) Leave the Online row out of the table without comment
Why: The answers are not missing at random: most online shoppers never met the staff. A grouped statistic always carries its n, and here the n changes what the number means.
A is wrong: It hides that the figure rests on 30% of the group, who may differ from the rest.
C is wrong: Inventing 576 answers makes the n look solid and adds no information.
D is wrong: Silently dropping a group hides the finding about who did not answer.

4.3 · Probability Distributions: self-check

Q7
You want to model the number of returned orders in a day: events scattered through time at a steady average rate, with no fixed number of tries. Which distribution do you try first, and what do you check?
A) Uniform; check that the histogram is flat
B) Normal; check that the 68-95-99.7 rule holds
C) Binomial with n = 731; check that p is the same every day
D) Poisson; check that the mean and the variance are about equal
Why: Counts of events in a fixed interval at a steady rate are Poisson, whose mean and variance are both the rate. For daily returns the data gives a mean of 1.564 and a variance of 1.526.
A is wrong: Zero, one and two returns are far more common than six or seven; nothing about the counts is flat.
B is wrong: The normal is continuous and symmetric. These are small counts that cannot go below zero.
C is wrong: 731 is the number of days in the data, not a number of tries within one day.
Q8
What does this print?
print(stats.norm(loc=20, scale=3).cdf(20))
0.5
Why: cdf(x) is the probability of x or less. A normal distribution is symmetric about its mean, so half of it lies at or below 20, whatever the SD.
Q9
batch = stats.binom(n=25, p=0.07) models returns in a batch of 25 orders. Which expression is the probability of 3 or more returns?
A) batch.sf(2)
B) batch.sf(3)
C) batch.pmf(3)
D) batch.cdf(3)
Why: sf(k) is the probability of more than k. For a count, "3 or more" is "more than 2".
B is wrong: sf(3) is more than 3: 4 or more. This off-by-one slip is the most common binomial error.
C is wrong: pmf(3) is exactly 3.
D is wrong: cdf(3) is 3 or fewer.
Q10
The standard deviation of order totals is about $178.76. What is the standard error of the mean of a random sample of 400 orders?
A) About $178.76
B) About $8.94
C) About $0.45
D) About $3,575
Why: SE = SD / sqrt(n) = 178.76 / 20, about $8.94. The SD describes how much single orders vary; the SE describes how much a sample mean would vary from sample to sample.
A is wrong: That is the SD. It does not shrink as you collect more data; the SE does.
C is wrong: That divides by n (400) instead of by the square root of n.
D is wrong: That multiplies by the square root of n.
Q11
From 200 orders an auditor gets a 95% confidence interval for the mean order total that is about $44 wide. Roughly how many orders are needed for an interval half as wide?
A) 100
B) 400
C) 800
D) 20,000
Why: The width is about 2 x t x SD / sqrt(n). To halve it, the square root of n must double, so n must be four times as large: 800. Precision is expensive.
A is wrong: A smaller sample gives a wider interval.
B is wrong: Doubling n shrinks the width by a factor of about 1.41, not 2.
D is wrong: That would shrink the width by a factor of 10, far more than asked.
Q12
What is true of the two lists this prints?
for _ in range(2):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    print(rng.integers(1, 7, size=3).tolist())
A) They are identical: each pass builds a new generator from the same seed, which replays the same numbers
B) They differ, because a generator never repeats itself
C) The second list continues where the first one stopped
D) The code raises an error, because a seed can be used only once
Why: A seed fixes the whole sequence. Re-creating the generator inside the loop restarts that sequence every time, so a simulation written this way repeats one experiment over and over. Create the generator once, at the top.
B is wrong: A seeded generator is reproducible by design.
C is wrong: That is what would happen if ONE generator were created before the loop.
D is wrong: A seed can be reused as often as you like.

Predict, Then Run: answers

Predict 4.2-1
One number. Careful: is the answer one of the values in the list?
print(pd.Series([10, 20, 30, 40, 50]).quantile(0.25))
20.0
Why: The 0.25 quantile sits a quarter of the way along the sorted values. With five values that is exactly the second one, 20. (With four values, [10, 20, 30, 40], the cut falls between two values and pandas interpolates: 17.5.)
Predict 4.2-2
Three z-scores. Predict the list.
s = pd.Series([2, 4, 6])
z = (s - s.mean()) / s.std()
print(z.tolist())
[-1.0, 0.0, 1.0]
Why: The mean is 4. The sample SD (pandas divides by n - 1) is the square root of (4 + 0 + 4) / 2, which is 2. So the values are one SD below, exactly at, and one SD above the mean.
Predict 4.3-1
Two numbers. No calculator needed.
model = stats.norm(loc=50, scale=10)
print(model.cdf(50), model.ppf(0.5))
0.5 50.0
Why: A normal distribution is symmetric about its mean, so half of it is at or below 50: cdf(50) is 0.5. ppf is the reverse of cdf: the value with half the distribution below it is 50.
Predict 4.3-2
Ten coin flips. Three numbers: predict each.
flips = stats.binom(n=10, p=0.5)
print(flips.mean(), flips.cdf(10), flips.sf(10))
5.0 1.0 0.0
Why: The expected count is n x p = 5. cdf(10) is the probability of 10 or fewer heads: certain. sf(10) is the probability of more than 10 heads in 10 flips: impossible.

The Lesson Pages' Own Quick Quizzes

Each lesson page ends with a five-question Quick Quiz (three options each). Learners do them at home; these are the correct options and the lesson's own reasons.

Lesson 4.2: Q1 C, Q2 A, Q3 B, Q4 B, Q5 A

Lesson 4.2 · Quiz 1
A column has a skewness of 2.4. Which description fits best?
C) Most values are low, with a long tail of large values on the right
Why: Positive skewness means the long tail is on the right. Most values are small, a few are much larger, and those few pull the mean above the median. Median and IQR are the safer summary.
Lesson 4.2 · Quiz 2
A store's visitor count on one day has a z-score of -2. What does that mean?
A) The count was two standard deviations below that store's average
Why: A z-score is a distance from the mean measured in standard deviations, with the sign giving the direction. Minus 2 is two standard deviations below that store's mean.
Lesson 4.2 · Quiz 3
In a standard box plot, where does the upper whisker end?
B) At the largest data value that is no more than 1.5 IQRs above Q3
Why: The fence is at Q3 + 1.5 x IQR, but the whisker is drawn only as far as the last actual data point inside that fence. Values beyond it are drawn as individual points.
Lesson 4.2 · Quiz 4
The IQR rule flags 4% of Pinecrest's orders. You check a sample and they are genuine large purchases. What should you do for a report on total revenue?
B) Keep them, because they are real and they are revenue
Why: A flag is a prompt to investigate. These values are real and belong to the question, so they stay. If you also describe a typical order, use the median and IQR so the tail does not distort it.
Lesson 4.2 · Quiz 5
What does this print?
answers = pd.Series([5, 5, 4, None, 1])
print(answers.mean(), answers.count())
A) 3.75 4
Why: pandas skips the missing answer: 15 divided by the 4 answers present is 3.75, and count() reports 4. The mean describes the people who answered, not all five who were asked.

Lesson 4.3: Q1 A, Q2 C, Q3 B, Q4 C, Q5 B

Lesson 4.3 · Quiz 1
A warehouse inspects 50 parcels. Each has the same 3% chance of being damaged, independently of the others. Which distribution models the number of damaged parcels?
A) Binomial, with n = 50 and p = 0.03
Why: A fixed number of independent tries (50), each with the same probability (0.03), counting successes: that is the definition of a binomial distribution with n = 50 and p = 0.03.
Lesson 4.3 · Quiz 2
Which value is closest to what this prints?
scores = stats.norm(loc=100, scale=15)
print(round(scores.cdf(130), 3))
C) 0.977
Why: 130 is 2 SDs above the mean. About 95 percent of a normal lies within 2 SDs, leaving 2.5 percent in each tail, so about 97.7 percent lies below 130.
Lesson 4.3 · Quiz 3
What does stats.norm(loc=100, scale=15).ppf(0.975) return?
B) The value that 97.5% of the distribution lies below, about 129.4
Why: ppf is the reverse of cdf: give it a probability and it returns the value with that much probability below it. Here that is 100 + 1.96 x 15, about 129.4.
Lesson 4.3 · Quiz 4
Order totals are strongly right-skewed. You repeatedly draw random samples of 200 orders and record each sample's mean. What does the central limit theorem say about those means?
C) The means are approximately normal, centered on the population mean, with SD about σ/√200
Why: The distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal curve centered on the population mean, with a standard deviation of sigma divided by the square root of n, whatever the shape of the population.
Lesson 4.3 · Quiz 5
From a random sample, you compute a 95% confidence interval for the mean order total: $178 to $222. Which statement is correct?
B) The method that produced this interval captures the true mean in about 95% of samples
Why: The 95 percent describes the procedure. Across many repeated samples, about 95 percent of intervals built this way contain the true mean. It is a statement about the mean, not about individual orders.

Warm-up and Exit Ticket Answers

Warm-up
  1. A figure with figsize=(10, 5) is saved with dpi=200. How many pixels wide and high is the PNG? Answer: 2,000 wide by 1,000 high: pixels = inches x dpi.
  2. The labels on your slide image are too small. Which two settings do you change, and what stays the same? Answer: A smaller figsize and a higher dpi, so the pixel size stays the same. Raising the dpi alone does not make text bigger.
  3. Pinecrest's mean order is $194.60 and the median is $146.85. Why do they differ, and which describes a typical order? Answer: The totals are right-skewed: a long tail of large orders pulls the mean up. The median describes a typical order; the mean is for totals.
  4. np.std(x) and pd.Series(x).std(): which divides by n and which by n - 1? Answer: NumPy divides by n (ddof=0); pandas divides by n - 1 (ddof=1).
  5. What is the IQR, and why prefer it to the SD for a skewed column? Answer: Q3 minus Q1, the width of the middle half of the data. It is robust: extreme values cannot inflate it.
Exit ticket
  1. The IQR rule flags 91 store-days as unusually high. What do you do before removing any of them? Answer: Investigate them: are they provable errors, and are they part of the question? (In the lesson's project all 91 are weekends, promotions or December days: real, and kept.)
  2. You have a frozen distribution called model. Which method returns the value that 95% of the distribution lies below? Answer: model.ppf(0.95). (cdf goes from a value to a probability; ppf goes back.)
  3. A 95% confidence interval for the mean order total is $162 to $204. Does that mean 95% of orders are between $162 and $204? If not, what does the 95% describe? Answer: No. It is a statement about the mean, not about single orders. The 95% describes the method: about 95 of 100 intervals built this way contain the true mean.

"The rule flagged them, so I deleted them."They treat an outlier rule as an error detector.Ask the two questions (provable error? part of the question?). Have them compute the share of revenue or the kinds of products they just removed.
Self-check Q3 answered "top 3.05%".They read a z-score as a percentile.Compute both for the same day: z and (revenue >= 12000).mean(). A z-score is a distance; rarity needs the shape.
np.percentile(totals, 0.9) used for the 90th percentile.They carry the pandas 0-to-1 scale into NumPy.Print it next to totals.quantile(0.9) and next to describe(). $13.50 cannot be the 90th percentile of orders with a median of $147.
A table of Likert means with no counts.They treat 1-to-5 answers as measurements, and a mean as self-explanatory.Add count and size. Ask for the share satisfied. Show the Online row: 248 answers from 824 respondents.
"The data becomes normal when the sample is big enough."A misreading of the central limit theorem.Plot 20,302 order totals: as skewed as ever. What becomes normal is the distribution of the sample mean.
Q9 answered batch.sf(3).They read sf(k) as "k or more".Have them print 1 - batch.cdf(2), batch.sf(2) and batch.sf(3) and say each in words.
Q10 answered $178.76 or $0.45.They confuse SD with SE, or divide by n instead of its square root.Rerun the central limit table: the SD of the means matches sigma / sqrt(n) at every n.
"95% of orders are in the confidence interval." / "There is a 95% chance the true mean is in it."They read the interval as a range for the data, or attach the probability to one interval instead of to the method.Compute the share of orders inside the interval (small). Rerun the coverage cell: the 95% is a percentage of intervals.
Different random numbers from the guide, and worry.They do not yet see a generator as a sequence.Restart the kernel and run all cells once, in order: the numbers match. Then run one cell twice and watch what follows change.
