Session 11: Shape, Outliers and Distributions

How to teach the session that finishes describing data and starts reasoning about data not yet seen. Before the break learners measure the shape of a column, locate values in it with percentiles and z-scores, run two outlier rules that disagree, and learn that a flag is a question, not a verdict. After the break they meet four workhorse distributions through scipy.stats, watch the central limit theorem turn a skew of 5.3 into a bell, and build their first confidence interval.

  • Type, do not paste. Every code block in the demo scripts is what to type, one notebook cell per step, in the course project folder. Every output shown was produced by running that code with the course environment (pandas 3.0, SciPy 1.18).

  • The lessons are the reference.   • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. Labs for this session live in labs/module-11/: starters for learners, solutions for you.
The risk of Session 11: the densest three hours of Module 4

Lesson 4.2 has eight ideas and Lesson 4.3 has more: four distributions, four methods, simulation, the central limit theorem, the standard error and a confidence interval. Nobody holds all of that after one pass. Decide in advance what must land in the room: before the break, a flag is a question, not a verdict, and every grouped number carries its n; after the break, freeze a distribution, ask it a question, check it against data, and the 95% describes the method. The rest is exposure that the lesson pages and the homework will consolidate. Say so to the room: "You will not remember all four distributions tonight. You will remember how to ask one a question." The second risk is random numbers: a learner who runs a cell twice gets different results from yours and thinks they made an error. Tell them in advance that the conclusions will match even when the digits do not.

Five retrieval questions (no notes, pairs, 60 seconds each)
  1. A figure with figsize=(10, 5) is saved with dpi=200. How many pixels wide and high is the PNG? Answer: 2,000 wide by 1,000 high: pixels = inches x dpi.
  2. The labels on your slide image are too small. Which two settings do you change, and what stays the same? Answer: A smaller figsize and a higher dpi, so the pixel size stays the same. Raising the dpi alone does not make text bigger.
  3. Pinecrest's mean order is $194.60 and the median is $146.85. Why do they differ, and which describes a typical order? Answer: The totals are right-skewed: a long tail of large orders pulls the mean up. The median describes a typical order; the mean is for totals.
  4. np.std(x) and pd.Series(x).std(): which divides by n and which by n - 1? Answer: NumPy divides by n (ddof=0); pandas divides by n - 1 (ddof=1).
  5. What is the IQR, and why prefer it to the SD for a skewed column? Answer: Q3 minus Q1, the width of the middle half of the data. It is robust: extreme values cannot inflate it.

Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Describe the shape of a distribution with a histogram and a skewness value, and say what the shape implies for your choice of summary
  • Locate a value within its column using percentiles, quantiles and z-scores
  • Apply the IQR and z-score outlier rules, explain why they disagree, and choose a defensible action for flagged values
  • Read every line of describe() and every part of a box plot
  • Summarize by group with groupby().agg(), and summarize 1-to-5 survey answers as the ordered categories they are
Mental model to say

Say: "Pour every value of a column onto a number line, like sand. The pile that forms is the distribution, and a histogram is a photograph of it. Center says where the pile sits. Spread says how wide it is. Shape says whether it is one even mound, a mound with a long ramp on one side, or two mounds. Every other summary is an attempt to describe that pile without showing the photograph."

Draw three piles on the board: an even mound, a mound with a ramp to the right, two mounds. Under them write the four words of the module so far: center, spread, shape, position. Last session covered the first two; today adds the other two and the question that follows from them: what do you do with the values that stand apart? Give the answer away early and repeat it all session: a flag is a question, not a verdict.

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Passing a percentage where a fraction is wanted, or the reverse. totals.quantile(25) raises ValueError: percentiles should all be in the interval [0, 1]; the dangerous twin raises nothing: np.percentile(totals, 0.9) quietly returns the 0.9th percentile. Coach: "Which function, and which scale does it take? pandas .quantile(): 0 to 1. NumPy np.percentile(): 0 to 100." Have them check a result against describe().
  • Deleting whatever the rule flags. "The IQR rule says they are outliers, so I removed them." Coach with two questions from the lesson's chart: "Is it a provable error? Is it part of your question?" Then make it concrete: the 848 flagged orders are 15.4% of revenue. Remove only provable errors, write down what was removed, and use the median and IQR so the rest cannot distort the summary.
  • A grouped statistic without its n, or a Likert mean on its own. A table of group means looks finished. Coach: "How many answers is each mean based on? Add count and size." For 1-to-5 answers: "Show me the distribution, or the share satisfied, next to that mean." A related silent failure worth naming: ax.boxplot() on a column with missing values draws an empty frame; dropna() first.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab (cold call, then pair-share):

  1. "Daily revenue has a skewness of 1.51. Which center and spread do you report, and what would you draw first?" Answer: Median and IQR (beyond 1 is strongly skewed), and a histogram first: skewness does not show how many humps there are.
  2. "A value is flagged by the IQR rule. What are the two questions you ask before doing anything?" Answer: Is it a provable error? Is it part of the question I am answering? Remove only provable errors; otherwise keep it (and stay robust) or analyze it separately and say so.
  3. "On a box plot, where does the upper whisker end?" Answer: At the largest data value that is still within 1.5 IQRs above Q3. Not at the fence itself, and not at the maximum unless nothing is flagged.

Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Explain what a random variable and a probability distribution are, and tell discrete from continuous
  • Choose between the uniform, normal, binomial and Poisson distributions for a situation, and check the choice against data
  • Answer probability questions with scipy.stats using pmf/pdf, cdf, sf and ppf
  • Simulate random outcomes reproducibly with np.random.default_rng and use simulation to check a calculation
  • Demonstrate the central limit theorem by simulation, compute a standard error, and build and correctly interpret a 95% confidence interval for a mean
Mental model to say

Say: "Picture a vending machine that drops a random snack each time you press the button. You cannot say what the next press will give you. But if someone tells you the machine is stocked with 50% chips, 30% candy and 20% cookies, you know a great deal: what to expect most often, how surprised to be by three cookies in a row, and roughly what 100 presses will produce. That stocking list is a probability distribution: every possible outcome, with its probability."

Then the two kinds, as two columns on the board: discrete (counted: returns in a day; binomial, Poisson; one exact value has a real probability) and continuous (measured: spend per visitor; uniform, normal; only ranges have probability). Finish the frame with why this lesson exists: "Every tool in the rest of this module, tests, A/B tests, confidence intervals, is built by asking 'what would chance alone produce?' Distributions are how that question gets an answer."

  5. Binomial: successes out of n tries (3m). Say: "A counting question. Out of the next 40 orders, how many will come back?" Fixed number of tries, independent, same probability each time.

  10. One sample, a standard error, an interval (4m). Say: "In real work you get one sample and never see the population. An auditor pulls 200 orders." 
    sample = rng.choice(population, size=200, replace=False)

    Write SE = s / sqrt(n) and mean plus or minus about 2 SE on the board.   11. What "95% confident" means (2m). Say: "The 95% describes the method, not this one interval. We own the population, so we can check: 10,000 samples, 10,000 intervals." 
    n = 200
big = rng.choice(population, size=(10_000, n))
m = big.mean(axis=1)
e = big.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
t_mult = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n - 1)

           & (population.mean() <= m + t_mult * e))

    93.9% of 10,000 intervals contain the true mean
    Close to 95%, a little short because the data is so skewed. Say exactly this: "If we repeated the whole procedure many times, about 95% of the intervals would contain the true mean. Any single interval either contains it or does not." And what an interval is not: it is not a range that holds 95% of the orders.
  12. Make the classic mistake on purpose (1m). Say: "scipy rarely complains. Watch." 
    wrong = stats.norm(loc=mu, scale=sigma ** 2)     # variance passed as scale
right = stats.norm(loc=mu, scale=sigma)
print(round(wrong.cdf(15), 3), round(right.cdf(15), 3))
    0.257 0.025
    scale is the standard deviation. Passing the variance makes the bell three times too wide, with no error: 25.7% instead of 2.5%. Textbooks write N(mean, variance), which is where the habit comes from.
Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Giving scipy the wrong meaning for an argument. scale=sigma ** 2 (the variance where the SD is wanted), or stats.uniform(10, 20) read as low and high when the second argument is the width (the range is 10 to 30). No error either way. Coach: "Name the arguments, loc= and scale=, and print model.ppf([0.025, 0.975]) to see whether the range is believable."
  • Off by one with sf on a count, or cdf and ppf swapped. "5 or more" is sf(4), not sf(5). And ppf(15) returns nan because ppf takes a probability and returns a value. Coach: "Say the question in words first: this or less, more than this, or which value has this much below it? Then pick the method."
  • Misreading a confidence interval. Two versions: "95% of orders are between $162 and $204" (it is a statement about the mean, not about single orders: only about a tenth of orders fall in such a range), and "there is a 95% probability the true mean is in this interval" (the 95% describes the method: about 95 of 100 intervals built this way contain the true mean). Coach by rerunning the coverage cell and asking: "What is the 95% a percentage of?"
