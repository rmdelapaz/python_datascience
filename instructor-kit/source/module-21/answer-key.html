Session 21 12 self-check items Lesson 8.1 Confidential

8.1 · The Pinecrest Report: self-check

Q1
Which of these is a capstone question rather than a topic?
A) Asheville store performance
B) An exploration of customer acquisition channels
C) Did products repriced on 2025-03-01 sell fewer units, March to December, than comparable products whose price did not change?
D) Everything about the WELCOME10 code
Why: It names what is compared with what, over which period, with a measure: someone could predict the notebook from it. That is the done-when test of Milestone 1.
A is wrong: A topic: no comparison, no measure, no decision.
B is wrong: Still a topic; "exploration" is a step, not an answer someone can act on.
D is wrong: Unbounded: it invites six comparisons and finishes none.
Q2
You compare a strongly right-skewed amount (customer spend) between two groups of about 500 people each. Which summary and interval suit it best?
A) The difference in medians with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval, reported next to the means
B) The difference in means with no interval, since the groups are large
C) Whichever of mean or median gives the smaller p-value
D) The maximum of each group, since large customers matter most
Why: Skew makes the mean sensitive to a few large values; the median describes the typical customer, and the bootstrap gives an interval without assuming a shape (Lesson 8.1, Exercise 2). Showing both is honest.
B is wrong: Large groups shrink the interval; they do not remove the need to report it.
C is wrong: Choosing the statistic by its p-value is searching until something is significant.
D is wrong: A maximum is one customer; it says nothing about the group.
Q3
What does this print?
orders = pd.DataFrame({"order_id": [1, 2], "order_total": [100.0, 50.0]})
lines = pd.DataFrame({"order_id": [1, 1, 1, 2], "sku": ["A", "B", "C", "A"]})
merged = orders.merge(lines, on="order_id")
print(len(merged), merged["order_total"].sum())
4 350.0
Why: Order 1 matches three lines, so its total of 100 is repeated three times: 300 + 50 = 350, for 150 of real revenue. This is the merge that multiplies rows (Common Mistake 2). Sum each quantity at the level where it lives, and print row counts around every merge.
Q4
Your main result: a difference of +1.2 percentage points (95% CI −1.5 to +4.0), p = 0.39. Which sentence belongs in the report?
A) The new approach works: the rate went up by 1.2 points
B) We found no clear difference: the data are consistent with anything from 1.5 points lower to 4.0 points higher, so a gain larger than about 4 points is unlikely
C) There is no difference between the groups
D) The result is not significant, so the analysis failed
Why: An honest null gives the estimate, the interval and what the interval rules out. It scores as well on the rubric as a dramatic discovery.
A is wrong: Reports the point estimate as if it were certain; the interval includes zero and negative values.
C is wrong: "No evidence of a difference" is not "no difference": the data cannot rule out a gain of up to about 4 points.
D is wrong: A well-measured null is a complete answer.
Q5
You compare how many customers "came back" after their first order. Some first orders were in November 2025 and the data end on 2025-12-31. What makes the comparison fair?
A) Count anyone who ever ordered again, whenever their first order was
B) Drop every customer whose first order was in 2025
C) Use months since signup as the outcome instead
D) Give everyone the same window (for example 180 days) and keep only customers whose first order was at least 180 days before the data end
Why: A fixed follow-up window gives every customer the same chance to return. Without it, a group with more late first orders looks worse for no reason except time.
A is wrong: Customers with late first orders have had little time: the measure mixes behavior with calendar position.
B is wrong: Throws away data without fixing the problem: 2024 first orders still differ in follow-up time.
C is wrong: Changes the question; time since signup is not coming back.
Q6
What does this print?
from itertools import combinations
channels = ["organic", "paid_search", "social", "referral", "in_store"]
print(len(list(combinations(channels, 2))))
10
Why: Five groups give 10 pairs. At a 5% threshold, comparing ten pairs of identical groups would produce a "significant" result about 40% of the time by chance, which is why the plan names the main comparison first and pairwise p-values are adjusted (for example with Holm's method) and their number reported.
Q7
Sales are seasonal and the company is growing. A change happened on a known date to some products only. Which comparison is fairest?
A) The change in the affected products, against the change in unaffected products over the same months a year earlier (a difference in differences)
B) The two months before the date against the ten months after it, for the affected products
C) Affected against unaffected products, after the date only
D) The year before against the year after, for all products together
Why: Comparing the same calendar months removes seasonality; comparing with products that did not change removes company growth. What is left is closer to the effect of the change, though the products were not chosen at random.
B is wrong: Before-and-after across seasons: January and February are slow months, so almost anything looks better afterward.
C is wrong: The two groups may have differed before the change; this cannot tell.
D is wrong: Mixes the affected products with the rest and keeps the growth.
Q8
Two conversion rates, 10% and 9%. What does this print? (It is Cohen's h, the effect size for two proportions.)
print(round(2 * np.arcsin(np.sqrt(0.10)) - 2 * np.arcsin(np.sqrt(0.09)), 3))
0.034
Why: h compares proportions on a scale where 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. One point on a 9% base is h of about 0.03: a very small effect, even if a large sample makes it significant. Report it next to the p-value.
Q9
Which caveat is specific enough to earn credit in the Communication row of the rubric?
A) More data is needed
B) Correlation is not causation
C) Customers chose whether to use the code, so those who used it may differ from those who did not in ways the data do not record
D) The results may not be accurate
Why: It names who was compared, why they may differ, and what the data cannot see. A reader learns exactly how far the claim goes.
A is wrong: The lesson names this as the generic caveat to avoid.
B is wrong: True everywhere and therefore says nothing about this analysis.
D is wrong: Vague: which results, inaccurate how, and why?
Q10
What is the first sentence after the question in report.md?
A) A description of the files you loaded
B) The answer, with the main number and its uncertainty
C) A list of the methods you used
D) A thank-you to the reader
Why: The reader asked a question. Leading with the answer is the template's first rule; everything the reader does not need in order to trust it stays in the notebook.
A is wrong: That belongs in the three-line Method section at the end.
C is wrong: A lab diary, the fifth common mistake.
D is wrong: Spends the reader's first seconds on nothing.
Q11
What does this print? (Run it twice: does the answer change?)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
print(rng.choice([10, 20, 30, 40], size=3).tolist())
[10, 40, 30]
Why: A seeded generator gives the same draws on every run, so a bootstrap interval or a sample in the notebook reproduces exactly after Restart and Run All. Unseeded randomness is one of the Developing-level faults in the Reproducibility row.
Q12
You have three minutes in the showcase. What do you say first?
A) How long the analysis took
B) Every chart you made while exploring
C) The cleaning steps, in order
D) The question, then the answer with its uncertainty, pointing at the one figure
Why: The showcase is the one-page report spoken aloud: question, answer, evidence, one caveat. The audience remembers the figure and the sentence.
A is wrong: Effort is not evidence.
B is wrong: Exploration charts are for you; one finished figure carries the argument.
C is wrong: The journey belongs in the notebook.

Predict, then run

P1
Prints:
first = pd.to_datetime(pd.Series(["2024-03-01", "2025-11-20"]))
print(((pd.Timestamp("2025-12-31") - first).dt.days >= 180).tolist())
[True, False]
Why: Only the first customer has 180 days of follow-up before the data end. The second cannot be judged on a 180-day window yet: keep them out of that comparison rather than counting them as "did not come back".
P2
Prints:
sales = pd.read_csv("data/daily_sales.csv", parse_dates=["date"])
by_month = sales.groupby(sales["date"].dt.month)["revenue"].mean()
print(by_month.loc[[2, 8, 12]].round(0).to_dict())
{2: 5551.0, 8: 8684.0, 12: 9281.0}
Why: Mean daily store revenue in August is far above February, and December is higher still. Any before-and-after comparison that crosses seasons measures the calendar first; compare the same months a year apart, or against a group that did not change.

The lesson page's own quiz

  1. C. Each order's total was repeated once per order line, so revenue was counted several times.
  2. B. With 20,000 per group a tiny difference reaches significance; d = 0.02 says it is very small.
  3. A. Online shoppers mostly skipped the question, so the mean describes mostly store shoppers.
  4. C. Staff are scheduled for expected demand, which also drives revenue; the correlation cannot show what adding staff would do.
  5. A. One static figure with a finding-first title in a one-page report suits a reader with a PDF and five minutes.

Lab Reference Solution

Lab 8.1 · labs/module-21/l8_1-capstone/capstone_scaffold_solution.py

Online orders paid by digital wallet were returned at 7.3% (n = 3,765) against 7.1% for card (n = 7,731): +0.2 points (95% CI −0.8 to +1.3), p = 0.66, h = +0.009. Without footwear 5.1% vs 4.8% (+0.3, CI −0.7 to +1.3); with footwear 14.7% vs 15.1% (−0.4, CI −3.2 to +2.6). Cancelled orders are excluded (they never shipped) and 998 gift-card orders are dropped by design; the table has 11,496 rows. The figure figure_1.png shows both subgroups with 95% error bars.

What it models: a plan written before code; overview() checks; one build_table() with row counts; one helper that produces all five parts of the sentence; a robustness check inside subgroups that are known to matter (footwear); a finding-first title; three plain sentences. What to accept from a learner: any correct comparison with all five parts and an honest reading. Common wrong turn: keeping cancelled orders in the denominator, which lowers both rates slightly and changes nothing important, but should be a stated decision.

Workbook Worked Example (for reference)

A strong sentence for a null result
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import confint_proportions_2indep, proportions_ztest

survey = pd.read_csv("data/survey.csv")
customers = pd.read_csv("data/customers.csv")
t = survey.merge(customers[["customer_id", "marketing_opt_in"]], on="customer_id",
                 validate="one_to_one").dropna(subset=["recommend"])
t["promoter"] = t["recommend"] >= 9
g = t.groupby("marketing_opt_in")["promoter"].agg(["sum", "count"])
yes, no = g.loc[True], g.loc[False]
p1, p0 = yes["sum"] / yes["count"], no["sum"] / no["count"]
z, p = proportions_ztest([yes["sum"], no["sum"]], [yes["count"], no["count"]])
low, high = confint_proportions_2indep(yes["sum"], yes["count"], no["sum"], no["count"])
h = 2 * np.arcsin(np.sqrt(p1)) - 2 * np.arcsin(np.sqrt(p0))
print(f"Promoters (recommend 9 or 10): {p1:.1%} of opted-in customers (n={yes['count']}) "
      f"against {p0:.1%} of the others (n={no['count']}), a difference of {(p1 - p0) * 100:+.1f} points "
      f"(95% CI {low * 100:+.1f} to {high * 100:+.1f}; z-test p = {p:.2f}; h = {h:.3f}).")
Promoters (recommend 9 or 10): 32.3% of opted-in customers (n=1020) against 31.3% of the others (n=726), a difference of +1.0 points (95% CI -3.5 to +5.4; z-test p = 0.66; h = 0.021).

Warm-up and Exit Ticket Answers

Warm-up
  1. Two Bokeh plots on one page: what must they share for a box selection in one to highlight the same rows in the other? Answer: The same ColumnDataSource object. (Sharing a range object links panning and zooming instead.)
  2. A Bokeh file saved with output_file has a dropdown. Which kind of callback works for a reader who opens the file? Answer: JavaScript: CustomJS (or js_link). A Python on_change callback needs a running Bokeh server.
  3. A reader moves a Streamlit slider. What runs? Answer: The whole script, from the top, with the slider returning its new value.
  4. Where does a value live that must survive reruns? And what stops a slow CSV load repeating on every click? Answer: st.session_state (per browser tab); @st.cache_data on the loading function (shared by all users).
  5. Your capstone: say the question letter and the headline comparison in one sentence: "I am comparing ___ with ___ using ___." Answer: No single answer. Listen for a comparison rather than a topic, and note who has none yet: they get the first coaching round.
Exit ticket
  1. Write your main result as one sentence with all five parts. Answer: Estimate for each group, the difference, its 95% interval, the test and p-value, and the effect size, in words a manager can repeat. A null result also says how large a difference the data can rule out.
  2. Name one caveat that is specific to your question and data (not "more data is needed"). Answer: Check it is specific: who or what is missing, what could not be separated, or why "because" is too strong for this design.
  3. What will you do in the 75 minutes of capstone work left after today, in order? Answer: Finish the figure, write report.md (Answer first, then Caveats), then Restart and Run All and check every number in the report against the fresh output, score yourself on the rubric, submit.

Common Wrong Answers and What They Reveal

  • Q2 answered B: believes large samples excuse a missing interval. Expect a report with bare means.
  • Q3 answered `2 150.0`: has not yet seen a merge repeat a value; check this learner's revenue totals for Questions B and D.
  • Q4 answered C: turns "no evidence" into "no difference". The single most common Communication fault in capstone reports.
  • Q5 answered A: the time-to-return trap. Ask the learner to print the latest first-order date in their comparison.
  • Q6 answered 5 or 20: counts groups, or ordered pairs. Ten unordered pairs.
  • Q7 answered B: the before-and-after trap; this learner needs the same-months comparison before submitting a Question D report.
  • Q9 answered B: a generic caveat. Ask: "true of this analysis in what way?"
  • Q11 answered "it changes": has not seen a seeded generator; check their bootstrap cells for a seed.
