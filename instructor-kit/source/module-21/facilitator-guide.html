Session 21: Capstone Studio and Showcase

How to run the last session: a studio where learners take their own capstone from a first finding to a quantified, visualized answer, a timed showcase, and the close of the course. The instructor answer notes and the rubric with weighting are in final-assessment.html.

1 lesson 3 hrs session Lesson 8.1 Session 21 of 21 Studio + showcase

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson, no new tools. Session 21 is a studio and a showcase. The lesson page is capstone_project.html (The Pinecrest Report, 420 minutes); learners arrive with Milestones 1 to 4 done, work Milestones 5 and 6 here, and finish 7 and 8 within a week.
  • Your instructor answer notes are in `../../final-assessment.html`: for each of the four questions the tables and steps of a sound analysis, the key numbers, the traps, what an honest null looks like (Questions B and C; for D, what an over-claim looks like), follow-up questions and what weak work gets wrong, plus the rubric with weighting and the submission rules. Read it before class. Nothing from it is said aloud as an answer or copied into a learner page: the lesson deliberately has no answer key.
  • Coach by asking. Every coaching move in this guide is a question. If a learner asks "is my answer right?", ask what would make it wrong and whether they checked.
  • The optional lab labs/module-21/l8_1-capstone/ holds a filled-in version of the lesson's notebook outline on a practice question that is not a capstone question. Point stuck learners at its structure, not its question.
The one risk of Session 21: a room of stuck people waiting for you

Twenty learners on four questions will want you all at once. The clock below rations your attention: a check-in every 15 minutes in which every learner reports a status in one word, then coaching for the reds first. Learners who are green help ambers at their table. Never sit with one learner for more than four minutes in a round.

Before the Session

  • ☐ Read final-assessment.html end to end, especially the notes for the questions your learners chose (the Session 20 sticky notes tell you the mix).
  • ☐ Run python3 labs/check_labs.py module-21: 2 passed, 0 failed.
  • ☐ Decide the showcase format from the size of the room (table below) and print the gallery-walk rotation cards or set up the second circle and its timekeeper.
  • ☐ Traffic-light cards (green, amber, red) or three colors of sticky note for each table; a visible countdown timer on the projector.
  • ☐ Projector: the rubric from the lesson page (The Rubric section) and the report template, both readable from the back.
  • ☐ No servers, no internet, no installs are needed today. Learners need their capstone folder with data/ beside the notebook.
  • ☐ Prepare the course-close slide content: what next (machine learning, open data, regression), the feedback link or form you use, the submission deadline (one week from today).

Warm-up

0:00

Warm-up: Retrieval from Session 20 and a Plan Check

10 min
Five questions (with answers)
  1. Two Bokeh plots on one page: what must they share for a box selection in one to highlight the same rows in the other? Answer: The same ColumnDataSource object. (Sharing a range object links panning and zooming instead.)
  2. A Bokeh file saved with output_file has a dropdown. Which kind of callback works for a reader who opens the file? Answer: JavaScript: CustomJS (or js_link). A Python on_change callback needs a running Bokeh server.
  3. A reader moves a Streamlit slider. What runs? Answer: The whole script, from the top, with the slider returning its new value.
  4. Where does a value live that must survive reruns? And what stops a slow CSV load repeating on every click? Answer: st.session_state (per browser tab); @st.cache_data on the loading function (shared by all users).
  5. Your capstone: say the question letter and the headline comparison in one sentence: "I am comparing ___ with ___ using ___." Answer: No single answer. Listen for a comparison rather than a topic, and note who has none yet: they get the first coaching round.
How to run it

Questions 1 to 4 on paper for four minutes, answers called out in two. Then question 5 around the room, ten seconds each: write the comparisons on the board under A, B, C, D. Anyone without a comparison is your first coaching visit in Studio 1.

Lesson Plan

8.1

The Pinecrest Report

175 min
Objectives (from the lesson page)
  • Turn a business question into a written analysis plan before touching the data
  • Carry an analysis from raw files to a tested, quantified answer, working independently
  • Report a result with its estimate, confidence interval, test, effect size and caveats
  • Present the answer in a one-page report with one or two figures that state their finding
  • Make the work reproducible, review it against a rubric, and share it responsibly
Frame · 10 min: the rubric and the strong sentence

Put the rubric on screen. Say: "Six rows. Proficient in all six is a successful capstone. Exemplary is what makes a reviewer remember your name. And a well-supported "no clear difference" scores exactly as well as a discovery." Then write the five-part sentence on the board, using the lesson's example: "On promotion days 25.8% of visitors bought, against 23.4% on comparable ordinary days: 2.4 points more (95% CI 1.9 to 2.9; Welch t-test p < 0.001; d = 0.93)." Label the five parts: two estimates, the difference, the interval, the test, the effect size. "By the break, your main result is a sentence like this one." Explain the clock: check-ins at fixed times, one word each, reds first.

Studio 1 · 45 min · Milestone 5, test and quantify
  • 0:00 stand-up check-in (3 min). Each learner holds up a card: green (the analysis table exists and I know my comparison), amber (table exists, method unclear), red (no table yet). Reds come to you first, ambers pair with a green at their table.
  • Coaching round 1 (12 min). Reds: "Show me one row of your analysis table and say what it is." If it does not exist, do Milestone 3 together in five minutes using the scaffold lab's build_table() pattern, then move on.
  • 0:15 check-in (2 min). One word each: "estimate", "interval", "test", "effect", "stuck". Coaching round 2 (13 min): stuck learners first; ask which method fits the data type (proportions, skewed amounts, several groups).
  • 0:30 check-in (2 min). Everyone tries to break their result: one robustness check (another definition, without the extremes, within each store or year). Coaching round 3 (10 min).
  • 0:42 (3 min). Everyone writes the main result as one five-part sentence in a Markdown cell. Spot-read four sentences aloud (with permission) and name the missing part, if any.
  • Done when: the main result is one sentence in the strong form, and at least one attempt to make it disappear is recorded (the lesson's own done-when).
Coaching notes by question (no answers aloud)

For each question: what strong work does, the traps to listen for, and three questions to ask. The numbers that go with these notes are in final-assessment.html.

Question A · Why is Asheville falling behind?
  • Strong work writes revenue as visitors × conversion × basket for each store and year, finds which factor moved, and shows whether the pattern is steady through the year (month by month against the same month a year earlier) and unusual against the other three stores.
  • Traps to listen for: reading the decline as "customers spend less" without checking; comparing seasons; treating 363 days as 363 independent customers; tying the October to November 2025 survey (one snapshot) to a two-year trend; using orders.csv (account holders only) as if it were the store total; ignoring the one missing Asheville revenue day.
  • Questions to ask: "Which factor moved, and by how much in each month?" "What do the other three stores' same factors do?" "What data would tell you why fewer people come in?"
Question B · Does it matter how we found the customer?
  • Strong work builds one row per customer (including those who never bought, with zero), defines "worth" before looking, compares five channels with a method that suits skewed spend, counts its pairwise comparisons, and makes the time window fair.
  • Traps to listen for: customers who signed up during 2024 and 2025 have had less time to spend; inner merges silently drop the 1,112 customers with no completed order (or keep the 739 orders of closed accounts with no channel); ten pairwise tests with no adjustment; "worth twice as much" answered from one ratio of means.
  • Questions to ask: "Does every customer in your comparison have the same amount of time?" "How many comparisons did you look at?" "How large a difference could your data have missed?"
Question C · Does the welcome discount bring people back?
  • Strong work defines a true first order (the data start on 2024-01-01, so a customer who signed up in 2019 has no first order in this file), uses a fixed follow-up window, compares two proportions with an interval and effect size, and words the result without "because".
  • Traps to listen for: the time-to-return trap (late first orders have had no time to come back); counting long-standing customers' first order in the data as a first order; customers chose to use the code (self-selection); counting cancelled first orders.
  • Questions to ask: "What is your follow-up window, and who is excluded by it?" "Whose first order is really a first order?" "What would a fair test of the code look like?"
Question D · What did the March 2025 price increase do to sales?
  • Strong work identifies the repriced products from order_items.unit_price (not today's products.price), compares units of repriced against unchanged products over the same months a year apart, checks the comparison before the change (a placebo period), reports the drop in units with its interval (about 14% fewer than trend), and states that the products were not chosen at random.
  • Traps to listen for: before-and-after across seasons (January and February are slow, so repriced products look busier afterward); company growth (repriced units flat year over year look like "no effect" until the unchanged products are beside them); valuing old orders at today's price; bulk lines and discontinued SKUs; category mix between the two groups. This question has a real effect, so also listen for over-claims: "sales fell 14%" for the whole company, or a price elasticity the data do not show.
  • Questions to ask: "How did you decide which products were repriced?" "What happened to the unchanged products over the same months, and before March?" "Is your number about units or revenue, and about which products?"
Studio 2 · 40 min · Milestone 6, then the Answer
  • 0:00 check-in (2 min). "Which one chart carries your answer?" Each learner names it in a sentence.
  • Work (18 min). Redraw that chart to the Module 3 standard: a title that states the finding, labeled axes with units, purposeful color, error bars identified in a caption, saved from code with savefig(..., dpi=150).
  • 0:20 check-in (3 min). Pairs swap screens for 60 seconds each: "Without reading anything but the figure, what is the answer?" If the partner cannot say, the title or the comparison needs work.
  • Work (12 min). Fix the figure; then draft the Answer paragraph of report.md: two or three sentences a manager could repeat, leading with the answer and the main number with its uncertainty.
  • 0:35 (5 min). Restart the kernel and Run All. Anyone whose notebook fails fixes it now with a neighbor; note their name for a follow-up message this week.
Showcase prep · 10 min

Each learner prepares the one figure and three sentences: the question, the answer with its uncertainty, one specific caveat. Lightning talk or gallery station, the content is the same. A learner with a null result (most often Question B or C) says so first and states what the interval rules out: model that sentence for the room once.

Checks for understanding

Workbook self-check, all twelve items, as homework with the submission (or as a five-minute pair activity if the showcase runs short).

Answers: Q1 C) Did products repriced on 2025-03-01 sell fewer units, March to December, than comparable products whose price did not change?; Q2 A) The difference in medians with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval, reported next to the means; Q3 4 350.0; Q4 B) We found no clear difference: the data are consistent with anything from 1.5 points lower to 4.0 points higher, so a gain larger than about 4 points is unlikely; Q5 D) Give everyone the same window (for example 180 days) and keep only customers whose first order was at least 180 days before the data end; Q6 10; Q7 A) The change in the affected products, against the change in unaffected products over the same months a year earlier (a difference in differences); Q8 0.034; Q9 C) Customers chose whether to use the code, so those who used it may differ from those who did not in ways the data do not record; Q10 B) The answer, with the main number and its uncertainty; Q11 [10, 40, 30]; Q12 D) The question, then the answer with its uncertainty, pointing at the one figure. Full set with explanations in the Answer Key.

Differentiation

Support: learners who arrive without Milestones 1 to 4 work from the scaffold lab's structure in Studio 1 and narrow their question to one comparison; they present a plan and a first finding in the showcase, and the rubric still applies at submission. Stretch: the Exemplary column: a pre-stated result that would change the reader's mind, a robustness check reported in the page, multiple comparisons or non-independence addressed, an alternative explanation tested; or the optional dashboard (adapt dashboard.py from Lesson 7.4) after the page is done.

Closing

Exit ticket · 5 min (answers)
  1. Write your main result as one sentence with all five parts. Answer: Estimate for each group, the difference, its 95% interval, the test and p-value, and the effect size, in words a manager can repeat. A null result also says how large a difference the data can rule out.
  2. Name one caveat that is specific to your question and data (not "more data is needed"). Answer: Check it is specific: who or what is missing, what could not be separated, or why "because" is too strong for this design.
  3. What will you do in the 75 minutes of capstone work left after today, in order? Answer: Finish the figure, write report.md (Answer first, then Caveats), then Restart and Run All and check every number in the report against the fresh output, score yourself on the rubric, submit.
Submission (within one week) and what goes home
  • After class: 75 minutes of required work. Finish the figure, write report.md from the template (Answer first, then Caveats), then Milestone 8: Restart and Run All, check every number in the report against the output, score yourself on the six rubric rows, work through the self-review checklist. Optional: a dashboard or interactive file (60 minutes), only after the page is done.
  • Hand in the project folder: pinecrest_report.ipynb (runs top to bottom with relative paths), one or two figures, report.md (one page), README.md (what this is, how to run it, where the data is from, and what help was used: AI assistants, web code, a classmate), and the self-scored rubric. Deadline: one week from today. The full rules, late options and how the two checkpoints are recorded are in final-assessment.html.
  • Bring list: there is no Session 22. For the submission: the course data/ folder is not handed in; link to it as the lesson's Sharing Your Work section says.

If You Run Long or Short

Long: shorten Showcase prep to five minutes and Course close to the journal prompt and the deadline; never cut the 0:42 strong-sentence write-up in Studio 1 or the Restart and Run All at the end of Studio 2. With 24 learners and lightning talks, switch to the gallery walk rather than cutting talks to one minute. Short: do the twelve self-check items in pairs; or run a second gallery round with the sticky-note questions answered in writing; or start Milestone 7 in the room (draft the Caveats section next, then Next questions).
