Random Number Generation

By the end you can
  • Create a random number generator with np.random.default_rng and draw floats, integers and arrays of any shape from it
  • Explain what a seed does, and structure your code so its random results can be reproduced
  • Sample from an array with and without replacement, shuffle it, and split rows at random
  • Draw from the normal, Poisson and binomial distributions and say what kind of quantity each one models
  • Estimate a probability by simulation, and judge how far to trust the estimate and the model behind it
Key ideas
  • Create one generator, once, with a seed: rng = np.random.default_rng(42). Draw everything from it.
  • A seed fixes where the sequence starts. The generator then moves forward with every draw, like a bookmark. Reproducible means: same code, run from the top, same result.
  • **rng.integers(low, high) leaves out the upper end.** A die is integers(1, 7). size can be a shape.
  • **rng.choice draws from your own values.** With replacement by default; replace=False gives different items; p sets the chances.
  • Sample row positions, then use them on every column, so parallel arrays stay matched.
  • **rng.permutation returns a shuffled copy. rng.shuffle works in place and returns None.**
  • Normal for measurements around a typical value, Poisson for counts of events, binomial for successes out of n tries.
  • Simulation: model, repeat, count. Describe one case with random draws, simulate many at once, take the mean of a mask.
  • More simulations make an estimate more precise. Only a better model makes it more true. Check the model against real data.

Worked example: how good is an average from only 40 days?

Suppose Asheville's door counter worked on only 40 days. How far from the truth could a 40-day average of visitors be? You have all 727 real days, so you can find out by sampling.

import numpy as np

def load(col, dtype=float):
    """Read one column of daily_sales.csv into an array."""
    return np.genfromtxt("data/daily_sales.csv", delimiter=",",
                         skip_header=1, usecols=col, dtype=dtype)

stores = load(1, dtype=str)
visitors = load(2)
asheville = visitors[stores == "Asheville"]
print(asheville.size, "days | true average", asheville.mean().round(1))
727 days | true average 274.9

The true average over all 727 days is 274.9 visitors. Normally you would not know it; here it lets you judge the samples.

rng = np.random.default_rng(21)

sample = rng.choice(asheville, size=40, replace=False)     # 40 different days
print(sample[:8])
print("estimate from 40 days:", sample.mean().round(1))
[238. 234. 173. 178. 403. 282. 199. 286.]
estimate from 40 days: 276.0

One sample of 40 different days gives one estimate. It is close, and a different sample would give a different number. How different?

many = rng.choice(asheville, size=(10_000, 40))            # 10,000 samples of 40 days, one per row
estimates = many.mean(axis=1)
low, high = np.percentile(estimates, [5, 95])
print(many.shape, estimates.shape)
print(f"90% of 40-day estimates fall between {low:.0f} and {high:.0f}")
(10000, 40) (10000,)
90% of 40-day estimates fall between 253 and 299

Ten thousand samples at once: size=(10_000, 40) is one sample per row, and mean(axis=1) collapses the 40 days. Nine times in ten, a 40-day average lands between 253 and 299: within about 23 visitors of the truth.

bigger = rng.choice(asheville, size=(10_000, 160)).mean(axis=1)
low, high = np.percentile(bigger, [5, 95])
print(f"90% of 160-day estimates fall between {low:.0f} and {high:.0f}")
print(estimates.std().round(1), bigger.std().round(1))
90% of 160-day estimates fall between 263 and 287
14.1 7.0

With four times as many days the range is half as wide (263 to 287), and the spread of the estimates halves from 14.1 to 7.0. More data helps, with diminishing returns. Module 4 gives this a name and a formula.

1.5

Linear Algebra Essentials

By the end you can
  • Compute a dot product and explain it as a weighted sum
  • Multiply matrices and vectors with @, predict the shape of the result, and tell @ apart from *
  • Solve a system of linear equations with np.linalg.solve and check the answer
  • Fit a line or a multi-feature linear model with np.linalg.lstsq, and interpret its coefficients and its error
  • Measure length and distance with np.linalg.norm, and standardize features before comparing them
Key ideas
  • A dot product multiplies matching positions and adds them up: a weighted sum. a @ b. Two vectors in, one number out.
  • matrix @ vector takes the dot product of every row with the vector: many totals, or many predictions, at once.
  • The shape rule: (n, k) @ (k, m) gives (n, m). The inner numbers must match, and order matters.
  • *** is position by position and keeps the shape. @ multiplies and adds.** When shapes broadcast, * gives the wrong thing with no error: check the shape.
  • **.T** swaps rows and columns.
  • **np.linalg.solve(A, b)** finds the unknowns of an exact system. Check with np.allclose(A @ x, b).
  • **np.linalg.lstsq(A, y)** finds the best-fitting weights when there are more equations than unknowns. That is linear regression. A column of ones gives the intercept; more features are more columns.
  • RMSE is the typical miss, in the units of what you predict. R squared is the share of the variation the model accounts for.
  • A fitted line describes; it does not prove. Read a coefficient as "goes with", with the other columns held fixed.
  • **np.linalg.norm** measures length; the norm of a difference is a distance. Standardize the columns first.

Worked example: which product is most like this one?

A shopper is looking at a pair of trail runners. Which other footwear product is most like it, judged by price and weight? products.csv has both columns (5 is price, 7 is weight in kilograms; 2 is the category and 3 the product type).

import numpy as np

def col(k, dtype=float):
    return np.genfromtxt("data/products.csv", delimiter=",", skip_header=1, usecols=k, dtype=dtype)

category, kind = col(2, dtype=str), col(3, dtype=str)
price, weight = col(5), col(7)

keep = (category == "Footwear") & ~np.isnan(weight)
shoes = np.column_stack([price[keep], weight[keep]])     # one row per product: price, weight_kg
kinds = kind[keep]
print(shoes.shape)
print(kinds[1], shoes[1])
(26, 2)
Trail Runners [135.95   0.57]

One mask (footwear, and a weight present) applied to every column keeps them parallel. np.column_stack puts price and weight side by side: 26 products, 2 features. Row 1 is a pair of trail runners at $135.95 and 0.57 kg.

raw = np.linalg.norm(shoes - shoes[1], axis=1)           # a table minus a row, then one norm per row
raw[1] = np.inf                                          # a product is not its own neighbor
print("closest on raw numbers:", kinds[raw.argmin()], shoes[raw.argmin()])
closest on raw numbers: Hiking Boots [135.95   1.21]

shoes - shoes[1] is a table minus a row, and axis=1 asks for one norm per row. The "closest" product is a pair of hiking boots: exactly the same price, and more than twice the weight. Price is measured in tens and hundreds, weight in fractions of a kilogram, so price decided everything.

z = (shoes - shoes.mean(axis=0)) / shoes.std(axis=0)     # every column on one scale
dist = np.linalg.norm(z - z[1], axis=1)
dist[1] = np.inf
print("closest after standardizing:", kinds[dist.argmin()], shoes[dist.argmin()])
closest after standardizing: Trail Runners [132.95   0.62]

After standardizing (the one-line step from Lesson 1.3), both columns pull equal weight, and the nearest product is another pair of trail runners: $3 cheaper and 50 grams heavier. That is what a shopper would call similar.

Glossary

**Generator (rng)**The object that produces random numbers: rng = np.random.default_rng(seed).
SeedA whole number that fixes where the generator's sequence starts, so results can be reproduced.
Pseudo-randomProduced by a fixed recipe, yet statistically indistinguishable from random for sampling and simulation.
With / without replacementWhether a drawn value can be drawn again. replace=False gives different items.
Sampling variationThe gap between what the probabilities say and what one particular sample shows.
DistributionA description of which values are likely: normal, Poisson, binomial, and many more.
Monte Carlo simulationAnswering a question by modeling one case with random draws, repeating it many times, and counting.
Law of large numbersThe average of more and more random draws settles toward the true underlying value.
Vector / matrixA 1-D array / a 2-D array.
Dot productMultiply matching positions and add them up: a weighted sum. Written a @ b.
**Transpose (.T)**The same matrix with rows and columns swapped.
Least squaresChoosing the weights that make the sum of squared misses as small as possible. Linear regression.
CoefficientA fitted weight: how much the prediction changes per unit of one feature, with the others held fixed.
ResidualAn actual value minus the model's prediction for it.
RMSERoot mean squared error: the typical size of a miss, in the units of what is predicted.
R squaredThe share of the variation that a model accounts for: 1 is a perfect fit, 0 no better than the mean.
Norm / distanceThe length of a vector / the norm of the difference between two vectors.
