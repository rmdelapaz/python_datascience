3.4 · Saving Figures: self-check

Q1
A figure is created with figsize=(9, 5) and saved with fig.savefig("sales.png", dpi=120). How many pixels is the image?
A) 900 wide by 500 high
B) 1,080 wide by 600 high
C) 600 wide by 1,080 high
D) 129 wide by 125 high
Why: pixels = inches x dpi, one dimension at a time: 9 x 120 = 1,080 wide and 5 x 120 = 600 high.
A is wrong: That is the size at the default 100 dpi; the call asks for 120.
C is wrong: figsize is (width, height), so the 9 belongs to the width.
D is wrong: dpi multiplies the inches. It is not added to them.
Q2
What does this print?
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4, 3))
fig.savefig("q2.png", dpi=50)
print(plt.imread("q2.png").shape[:2])
(150, 200)
Why: plt.imread returns an array shaped (rows, columns, channels), so the height comes first: 3 x 50 = 150 rows and 4 x 50 = 200 columns. A common wrong answer is (200, 150): width first, the way figsize is written.
Q3
Two PNG files are both 1,600 by 800 pixels. File A came from figsize=(16, 8) at dpi=100, file B from figsize=(8, 4) at dpi=200. Which statement is true?
A) The two images are identical, because the pixel counts are identical
B) B is blurrier, because its sheet was smaller
C) A holds more detail, because its figure was larger
D) In B the text, lines and markers are twice as large relative to the chart
Why: Text and line widths are measured in points (1/72 inch), so they take up twice the share of an 8-inch sheet that they take of a 16-inch sheet. The dpi then scales the whole sheet to the same 1,600 pixels.
A is wrong: Same pixel count, different drawing: the sheet size decided how big the text is on the sheet.
B is wrong: Both files have exactly 1,600 by 800 pixels; neither is blurrier.
C is wrong: Detail in a PNG is its pixel count, and the counts are equal.
Q4
A scatter plot of 200,000 points must go into a printed report as a PDF. The file is huge and slow to open. What is the best fix?
A) Pass rasterized=True to the scatter layer and keep saving as PDF
B) Save as JPEG instead, because JPEG files are small
C) Lower the dpi of the PDF to 50
D) Add bbox_inches="tight" to the save call
Why: A vector file stores one drawing instruction per dot. Rasterizing only the heavy layer turns the dots into an embedded image, while the axes, title and labels stay sharp vector text.
B is wrong: JPEG smears sharp edges and text; the lesson says never to use it for charts.
C is wrong: Vector marks have no dpi, so the 200,000 instructions are all still there.
D is wrong: That changes the saved area, not the number of marks in the file.
Q5
A chart is saved with transparent=True and pasted onto a dark slide. The title and tick labels cannot be seen. Why?
A) Transparent PNG files cannot contain text
B) The slide program removed the labels when it resized the image
C) Only the background became transparent; the text is still black, on a dark slide
D) The dpi was too low for the labels to be drawn
Why: transparent=True removes the figure's background and nothing else. Black labels on a dark page disappear. Draw the chart in a dark style, or save with a solid facecolor="white".
A is wrong: The text is in the file; it is simply the same color as what is behind it.
B is wrong: Resizing does not delete parts of an image.
D is wrong: A low dpi gives fewer pixels, not missing labels.
Q6
What does this print?
plt.close("all")
first, ax = plt.subplots()
second, ax = plt.subplots()
first.savefig("q6.png")
plt.close(first)
print(len(plt.get_fignums()))
1
Why: Saving a figure does not close it. Two figures were created and one was closed, so pyplot is still holding second. In a loop that makes hundreds of charts this is how memory fills up: create, draw, save, close.

4.1 · Descriptive Statistics I: Center and Spread: self-check

Q7
What does this print?
print(pd.Series([10, 20, 30, 40, 400]).median())
A) 100.0
B) 30.0
C) 25.0
D) 400
Why: The median is the middle value of the sorted list. With five values that is the third one, 30. The 400 could be 4,000,000 and the answer would not change.
A is wrong: That is the mean (500 / 5): the balance point, dragged up by the 400.
C is wrong: That is the mean of the two values next to the middle of the first four; there are five values, so there is a single middle.
D is wrong: That is the maximum.
Q8
orders["payment_method"] holds the words card, digital_wallet, cash and gift_card. Which summary of its center makes sense?
A) The mean
B) The median
C) The mode
D) The standard deviation
Why: Categories cannot be added or put in order, so the only center is the most common value: .mode() (card, with 12,392 of the 20,302 orders).
A is wrong: .mean() on text raises TypeError: Cannot perform reduction 'mean' with string dtype.
B is wrong: A median needs an order from smallest to largest; payment methods have none.
D is wrong: That is a measure of spread, and it needs numbers.
Q9
What does this print?
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
print(np.var(x), pd.Series(x).var())
2.0 2.5
Why: The squared deviations from the mean (3) are 4, 1, 0, 1, 4: a sum of 10. NumPy divides by n (10 / 5 = 2.0, ddof=0); pandas divides by n - 1 (10 / 4 = 2.5, ddof=1).
Q10
Pinecrest's order totals have a mean of $194.60, a median of $146.85 and a mode of $53.90. Finance wants a revenue forecast for 1,000 orders next month. Which number do you multiply by 1,000?
A) The mean, $194.60
B) The median, $146.85
C) The mode, $53.90
D) The midpoint of the mean and the median
Why: Only the mean multiplies back to a total: mean x count = sum. The median describes a typical order, and it would understate revenue by about a quarter here.
B is wrong: 20,302 orders x $146.85 is about $2.98 million; the real total is $3.95 million.
C is wrong: The mode of a measured quantity is mostly an accident: $53.90 is 114 of 20,302 orders.
D is wrong: An invented number that is neither the typical order nor the total divided by the count.
Q11
One order in a column of 20,000 order totals was typed as $1,000,000 by mistake. Which pair of summaries barely changes?
A) Mean and standard deviation
B) Range and standard deviation
C) Mean and range
D) Median and interquartile range
Why: The median and the IQR depend only on the order of the values near the middle. One extreme row is just "one more value above the middle". They are robust.
A is wrong: Both are built from every value's distance, so one huge value has huge leverage: in the lesson the SD went from $178.76 to $7,019.02.
B is wrong: The range IS the two extremes, and the SD squares the distance to the bad row.
C is wrong: The mean rose by $49 in the lesson's stress test, and the range became $999,993.62.
Q12
What does this print?
revenue = pd.Series([100.0, np.nan, 300.0])
print(revenue.mean(), np.mean(revenue.to_numpy()), revenue.count())
A) 200.0 nan 2
B) 133.33 nan 3
C) 200.0 200.0 3
D) nan nan 2
Why: pandas skips missing values (skipna=True), so its mean is (100 + 300) / 2. NumPy's np.mean does not skip: one nan makes the answer nan, with no error. .count() counts the values that are present.
B is wrong: pandas does not treat the missing value as 0, and .count() leaves it out.
C is wrong: np.mean returns nan; np.nanmean is the NumPy function that skips.
D is wrong: The pandas method skips the missing value instead of spreading it.

Predict, Then Run: answers

Predict 3.4-1
What does this print? (Careful: which number comes first?)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5, 2))
fig.savefig("charts/p1.png", dpi=300)
plt.close(fig)
print(plt.imread("charts/p1.png").shape[:2])
(600, 1500)
Why: shape is (rows, columns): the height comes first. 2 inches x 300 dpi = 600 rows, and 5 x 300 = 1,500 columns.
Predict 3.4-2
What two numbers does this print?
for i in range(3):
    fig, ax = plt.subplots()
    fig.savefig(f"charts/p2_{i}.png")
print(len(plt.get_fignums()))
plt.close("all")
print(len(plt.get_fignums()))
3
0
Why: Saving does not close a figure, so after the loop pyplot is still holding all three. plt.close("all") releases them. In your own loops, close each figure right after saving it.
Predict 4.1-1
Three numbers: the mean, the median and the mode. Predict all three.
s = pd.Series([3, 5, 5, 8, 50])
print(s.mean(), s.median(), s.mode()[0])
14.2 5.0 5
Why: The sum is 71, so the mean is 14.2: larger than four of the five values, because the 50 has leverage. The middle value of the sorted list is 5, and 5 is also the only value that appears twice.
Predict 4.1-2
Same three numbers, two libraries. What does each print?
x = np.array([2, 4, 9])
print(np.var(x), pd.Series(x).var())
8.666666666666666 13.0
Why: The mean is 5 and the squared deviations are 9, 1 and 16: a sum of 26. NumPy divides by n = 3 (8.67); pandas divides by n - 1 = 2 (13.0). With only three values the two differ by a third.

A figure is created with figsize=(8, 4) and saved with fig.savefig("chart.png", dpi=150). How large is the image?
C) 1,200 by 600 pixels

quiz_fig, _ = revenue_chart("Bend", figsize=(6, 3))

Lesson 3.4 · Quiz 3
A line chart is going into a printed annual report, and the designer may need to resize it during layout. Which format is the best choice?
A) PDF or SVG, because vector files stay sharp at any size
Why: A vector file stores drawing instructions instead of pixels, so it stays sharp at any size the designer chooses, and its text remains real text.
Lesson 3.4 · Quiz 4
A chart saved from figsize=(16, 9) at dpi=120 fills a slide exactly (1,920 by 1,080), but nobody can read the labels. What is the best fix?
B) Use figsize=(8, 4.5) at dpi=240
Why: Text is sized in points, a fixed fraction of an inch, so on a smaller sheet it takes up more of the chart. Halving the figure size and doubling the dpi keeps 1920 by 1080 pixels and makes the text twice as large.
Lesson 3.4 · Quiz 5
A script saves 300 charts in a loop and gets slower and slower until it runs out of memory. What is missing?
A) plt.close(fig) after each savefig
Why: pyplot keeps every figure it creates until it is closed. Closing each figure after saving it releases its memory, so the loop uses the same small amount for every chart.

Lesson 4.1: Q1 B, Q2 A, Q3 C, Q4 A, Q5 C

Lesson 4.1 · Quiz 1
Pinecrest's order totals have a mean of $194.60 and a median of $146.85. What is the best explanation?
B) A long tail of large orders pulls the mean up, while the median stays with the bulk of the orders
Why: The distribution is right-skewed. Large orders have leverage on the mean, the balance point, but each of them counts as just one value above the middle for the median.
Lesson 4.1 · Quiz 2
What does this print?
ratings = np.array([2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9])
print(np.std(ratings))
A) 2.0, because NumPy divides the sum of squared deviations by n
Why: The mean is 5 and the squared deviations add up to 32. NumPy divides by n, which is 8, giving a variance of 4 and a standard deviation of 2.0. pandas would divide by 7 and print about 2.14.
Lesson 4.1 · Quiz 3
A column of delivery times is strongly right-skewed and may contain a few data-entry errors. Which pair of summaries describes it most faithfully?
C) Median and interquartile range
Why: The median and the IQR depend only on the order of the values near the middle, so a long tail or a few bad rows barely move them.
Lesson 4.1 · Quiz 4
What does this print?
pay = pd.Series(["card", "cash", "card", "gift_card", "cash"])
print(pay.mode().tolist())
A) ['card', 'cash']
Why: card and cash both appear twice, so there is a tie, and mode() returns a Series containing every winner, sorted.
Lesson 4.1 · Quiz 5
Store A took 10 orders with a mean of $100. Store B took 90 orders with a mean of $200. What is the mean of all 100 orders?
C) $190
Why: Total dollars are 10 x 100 + 90 x 200 = 19,000 over 100 orders, so the mean is 190. Group means must be weighted by group size, never averaged directly.

Warm-up and Exit Ticket Answers

Warm-up
  1. fig, axs = plt.subplots(2, 2): what kind of object is axs, and what loop visits every panel whatever the grid shape? Answer: A 2-D NumPy array of Axes. for ax, group in zip(axs.flat, groups): works for every grid shape.
  2. What does sharey=True buy you in a grid of panels, and what does it cost? Answer: Heights become comparable across panels. The cost: small series are flattened, so shape is harder to see. Choose on purpose and tell the reader.
  3. Why is a twin-axis chart with two different measures risky, and what should you draw instead? Answer: The two scales are arbitrary, so the chart can be made to say almost anything. Use stacked panels with a shared x axis, or plot a ratio.
  4. What should a finished chart's title say? And the subtitle? Answer: The title states the finding. The subtitle says what is plotted, the units and the period.
  5. You have a dictionary of style settings. How do you apply it to one block of plotting code without changing the rest of the session? Answer: with plt.rc_context(style_dict): around the block.
Exit ticket
  1. A figure has figsize=(6, 4) and is saved with dpi=150. How many pixels wide and high is the PNG? Answer: 900 wide by 600 high (6 x 150 and 4 x 150).
  2. A column of order totals is strongly right-skewed. Which measure of center and which measure of spread do you report for a typical order? Answer: The median and the IQR. (The mean is for totals.)
  3. np.std(x) and pd.Series(x).std() give different answers for the same small list. Why? Answer: NumPy divides by n (ddof=0); pandas divides by n - 1 (ddof=1).
