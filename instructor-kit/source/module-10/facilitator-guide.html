Session 10: Saving Figures; Center and Spread

How to teach the session where Module 3 ends and Module 4 begins. Before the break learners get their charts out of the notebook: they predict a saved figure's size from figsize and dpi, find out why text comes out tiny, choose between PNG, SVG and PDF, and build one preset-driven save function. After the break they start statistics with its two most used ideas, center and spread, and discover that Pinecrest's "average order" of $194.60 describes almost nobody.

How to Use This Guide

  • Same rhythm as every session: Frame (the goal and a mental model) → Live demo (you type, they type along) → Guided lab (the kit's lab files) → Start the lesson project (so nobody opens it cold at home). The lesson's exercises, quiz and journal are homework.
  • Type, do not paste. Every code block in the demo scripts is what to type, in a Jupyter notebook in the course project folder, one cell per step. The one exception is the setup cell of 3.4, which learners may copy from the lesson page. Every output shown was produced by running that code with the course environment (pandas 3.0, Matplotlib 3.11).
  • Ask for a prediction before you run. Both lessons are full of results that surprise people (same pixels, different chart; 63% of orders below the average order). The surprise only teaches if they committed to a guess first.
  • The lessons are the reference. Keep matplotlib_saving_figures.html and statistics_descriptive_part1.html open in browser tabs for the figures (the two same-pixel charts, the raster zoom, the histogram with mean and median) and the tables (destinations, formats, pairing center with spread).
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. Labs for this session live in labs/module-10/: starters for learners, solutions for you.
The risk of Session 10: two lessons whose mistakes raise no error

A chart saved at the wrong size, a blank PNG from plt.savefig(), a mean reported for skewed data, an SD that silently used the other ddof: none of these stop the program. Train one habit in each half. Before the break: do not trust, check (plt.imread(path).shape, then open the file). After the break: every mean gets a median next to it, and every summary gets its .count(). The second risk is the change of subject at the break. Some learners arrive nervous about statistics; say plainly that today has no formulas to memorize and that every number is one method call.

Five retrieval questions (no notes, pairs, 60 seconds each)
  1. fig, axs = plt.subplots(2, 2): what kind of object is axs, and what loop visits every panel whatever the grid shape? Answer: A 2-D NumPy array of Axes. for ax, group in zip(axs.flat, groups): works for every grid shape.
  2. What does sharey=True buy you in a grid of panels, and what does it cost? Answer: Heights become comparable across panels. The cost: small series are flattened, so shape is harder to see. Choose on purpose and tell the reader.
  3. Why is a twin-axis chart with two different measures risky, and what should you draw instead? Answer: The two scales are arbitrary, so the chart can be made to say almost anything. Use stacked panels with a shared x axis, or plot a ratio.
  4. What should a finished chart's title say? And the subtitle? Answer: The title states the finding. The subtitle says what is plotted, the units and the period.
  5. You have a dictionary of style settings. How do you apply it to one block of plotting code without changing the rest of the session? Answer: with plt.rc_context(style_dict): around the block.

Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Save a figure with fig.savefig() and predict its size in pixels from figsize and dpi
  • Choose between raster formats (PNG) and vector formats (SVG, PDF) for a given destination
  • Explain why text looks tiny or huge in a saved chart, and fix it with the figure size
  • Control the saved area and background with bbox_inches, facecolor and transparent
  • Write one function that applies a house style and saves for web, slides or print, then use it in a loop that closes each figure
Mental model to say

Say: "A Matplotlib Figure is a sheet of paper with a size in inches. Everything on it, including the text, is laid out on that sheet. Saving to an image is like scanning the sheet: the scanner's resolution, in dots per inch, decides how many pixels you get. A bigger sheet and a finer scan both give more pixels, but they are not the same thing. A finer scan makes everything sharper. A bigger sheet makes the text smaller relative to the page."

Hold up a sheet of paper with one word written on it, then a sheet twice the size with the same word at the same handwriting size. "Same pen. Which page looks empty?" Write on the board and leave it up all session: pixels = inches x dpi and under it figsize sets how big the text looks; dpi sets how many pixels you get. The lesson's decision chart (printed or resized: PDF or SVG; a slide: PNG at slide size; web or email: PNG at about 110 dpi) is worth showing from the lesson page once.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

                            values="revenue", aggfunc="sum")

def thousands(x, pos):
    return f"${x / 1000:,.0f}K"

def revenue_chart(store, figsize=(8, 4)):
    """Draw one store's monthly revenue. Returns the figure and its Axes."""
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=figsize, layout="constrained")
    ax.plot(monthly.index, monthly[store], color="#667eea",
            marker="o", markersize=3)
    ax.set_title(f"{store}: monthly in-store revenue",
                 loc="left", fontweight="bold")
    ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter(FuncFormatter(thousands))
    ax.set_ylim(bottom=0)
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
    return fig, ax

  8. Put the decisions in one place (4m). Say: "I know the settings now. Typing them correctly every time is how mistakes creep in. One table, two small functions." Type the table slowly and say each pixel size out loud as you type the row. 
    PRESETS = {
    "web":   {"figsize": (9, 4.5),   "dpi": 110, "extension": "png"},
    "slide": {"figsize": (9.6, 5.4), "dpi": 200, "extension": "png"},
    "print": {"figsize": (6.5, 3.5), "dpi": 300, "extension": "pdf"},
}

def new_figure(kind="web"):
    """Create a figure sized for 'web', 'slide' or 'print'."""
    size = PRESETS[kind]["figsize"]
    return plt.subplots(figsize=size, layout="constrained")

def save_chart(fig, name, kind="web", folder="charts"):
    """Save fig as folder/name_kind.ext. Returns the path."""
    preset = PRESETS[kind]
    folder = Path(folder)
    folder.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    path = folder / f"{name}_{kind}.{preset['extension']}"
    fig.savefig(path, dpi=preset["dpi"], facecolor="white")
    return path

    ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter(FuncFormatter(thousands))
    ax.set_ylim(bottom=0)

for kind, preset in PRESETS.items():
    width, height = preset["figsize"]
    dpi = preset["dpi"]
    print(kind, round(width * dpi), "x", round(height * dpi))

slide 1920 x 1080
print 1950 x 1050

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Raising dpi to fix small text. The slide's labels are tiny, so the learner saves again at dpi=600: a file many times larger with text exactly as small. Coach with the board rule: "Which knob changes how big the text is on the sheet?" Then have them halve the figsize and double the dpi, and compare plt.imread(path).shape before and after: same pixels, bigger text.
  • plt.savefig() instead of fig.savefig() (often after plt.show() in a script, or after another figure was created). No error, a blank or wrong file. Coach: "Which figure did you ask to be saved? Show me its name in that line." The check that catches it is the lesson's: bool((plt.imread(path)[..., :3] == 1).all()).
  • Reading imread().shape as (width, height), or expecting an exact size with bbox_inches="tight". Coach the first with "rows first, like a DataFrame: shape is (rows, columns)". For the second, ask them to print the shape with and without the argument (800 x 400 against 811 x 411 in the demo) and decide whether their destination needs an exact size.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab (cold call, then pair-share):

  1. "I need an image 1,000 pixels wide. Give me two different figsize and dpi pairs. How will the two files differ?" Answer: For example 10 inches at 100 dpi and 5 inches at 200 dpi. Same pixels; in the 5-inch version the text and lines are twice as large relative to the chart.
  2. "The designer will resize my chart for the annual report. Which format, and why?" Answer: PDF or SVG: vector files are redrawn sharply at any size. A PNG only has the pixels it was saved with.
  3. "My loop saves 300 charts and the kernel dies. What is missing?" Answer: plt.close(fig) after each save. pyplot keeps every figure alive until it is closed.

Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Compute the mean, median and mode of a column with pandas and say what each one measures

  • Compute range, variance, standard deviation and interquartile range (IQR), and explain each in the units of the data

Mental model to say

Say: "Nobody reads 1,000 reviews before choosing a restaurant. You look at the average rating, and then, if you are careful, at how much the reviews disagree. A 4.2 where everyone gave a 4 is a different restaurant from a 4.2 made of fives and ones. Two numbers, what is typical and how much it varies, are the least you need to describe anything honestly."

Name the two questions and write them as column headings on the board: Center: what is typical? (mean, median, mode) and Spread: how much does it vary? (range, variance, SD, IQR). Fill the columns in as the demo goes. Open Module 4 in one sentence: "Modules 1 to 3 gave you tables and charts. This module turns them into claims you can defend." Then the hook: "'Our average order is $195' is true. And most orders are nowhere near $195. By the break after next you will be able to say why."

  1. The mean is a balance point (3m). New notebook. Say: "The marketing lead wants the average order value for a slide. Easy: add them up, divide by how many." 
    import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Two libraries, two standard deviations. A learner's SD does not match a neighbor's or the lesson's, because one used np.std (divide by n) and the other .std() on a Series (divide by n - 1). Coach: "When your number does not match someone else's, check ddof first." Have them write the choice into the code, np.std(x, ddof=1), so it stops being an accident.
  • Averaging averages. To get an overall mean they average the group means from a groupby (the lesson's example gives $182.66 instead of $194.60). Coach: "Does Asheville's 1,413 orders count as much as Online's 12,854 in your number?" Fix: compute from the original rows, or np.average(means, weights=counts).
  • Mean and SD for skewed data out of habit. "Orders average $195 plus or minus $179" is arithmetic that paints a symmetric hump that does not exist. There is no error message, so it is the most common. Coach with one question every time a mean is reported: "What is the median? Show me the histogram." If mean and median disagree noticeably, lead with median and IQR.
