Session 5: Cleaning and Grouping

How to teach the session where the file fights back and then starts answering questions. First learners audit a damaged export of 20,515 rows and repair it one documented step at a time: structure, text, numbers, dates, missing values, and prove the result. Then they learn split-apply-combine: groupby with named aggregation, transform and filter, and the quiet traps that return a plausible wrong number.

The #1 risk of Session 5: plausible wrong numbers

Both lessons are about results that look fine and are not. In 2.3 it is the value that converts without complaint: a total 100 times too large, a dropna() that keeps 2,085 rows of 20,302, a fillna(0) that says 40 customers paid nothing. In 2.4 it is the groupby that drops 18,154 rows because their key is missing, a count that should have been a size, an average of averages. None of these raises an error. The habit to train all session: "Count the rows before and after. Do the groups add up?" The second risk is time: the 2.3 demo is long. If you are past 0:50 at the dates step, show the three-format idea in one cell and move on.

Warm-up: Retrieval from Session 4

10 min
Five retrieval questions (no notes, 5 minutes)

Slide 3. Paper only, notebooks closed, then one minute with a neighbor. Take answers quickly; the fifth question leads into today.

  1. What three things make up a Series? Answer: An array of values, an index of labels, and an optional name.
  2. On a table with the default index, how many rows do df.loc[0:3] and df.iloc[0:3] return? Answer: 4 and 3: a label slice includes its end, a position slice leaves out the stop.
  3. df[df["price"] > 100]["price"] = 0 changes nothing. What do you write instead? Answer: df.loc[df["price"] > 100, "price"] = 0: one .loc on the table you want to change.
  4. A column of birth years loads as float64 and prints 1989.0. Why, and what is the fix? Answer: Some years are missing and NumPy's int64 cannot hold a missing value. Load it as "Int64" (capital I).
  5. After pd.read_excel(...) the columns are called Unnamed: 1, Unnamed: 2. What happened, and what do you check after every load? Answer: The header is not on the row pandas read as the header (skiprows / header). After every load: shape, dtypes, head().

Mental model to say

Say: "Every Pinecrest store has a repair bench for rental tents that come back damaged. The technician does the same thing every time. Inspect the whole tent and write down every problem before fixing any. Repair one thing at a time, structural damage first: poles, then seams, then small holes. Check each repair before starting the next. Never throw away the damage report. And some stains do not come out: the honest move is to note them on the tag, not to paint over them. Cleaning data is that routine. The raw file is the tent as it came back, and you never alter it. Your code is the repair log." Write the loop on the board and leave it up: audit → pick one problem → fix it in code, on a copy → check → next. Under it, the order: structure, text, numbers, dates, missing values.

Three mistakes to expect
  • Fixing before looking, or in the wrong order. Symptoms: KeyError: 'Customer ID' (the invisible leading space), ValueError: could not convert string to float: '$107.90' (converting before cleaning), an astype("int64") that fails because the TOTAL row is still there. Coach: "What did your audit say about that column? What is on your repair list above this item?" Have them print df.columns.tolist() and fix structure before contents. Each of these error messages quotes the exact value that broke the rule: teach them to read it as a finding.
  • Case without whitespace, or a mapping written from memory. Symptoms: nunique() barely falls after .str.title(); a mapping key such as "Bend OR" that never matches because title case already turned it into Bend Or; .str.replace("$", "", regex=True) that changes nothing. Coach: "Print value_counts() after the mechanical steps and build the dictionary from what you see, not from what you remember." Always .str.strip() first.
  • Making the NaNs go away. Symptoms: dropna() with no arguments (2,085 rows left of 20,302, no warning); fillna(0) on a money column; df["col"].fillna(..., inplace=True), which warns with ChainedAssignmentError and fills nothing. Coach: "What are you claiming about those 40 customers when you write 0? How many rows did you have before and after that line?" The rule to repeat: a missing value is a decision, and the decision gets written down.

Checks for understanding
  1. Why is the raw file never edited by hand? Answer: A hand edit leaves no record, cannot be reviewed and must be redone for every new export. Every fix is a line of code on a copy.
  2. What does the distinct column of the audit tell you about a category column? Answer: Whether it has the number of values the business has. 31 store names for 5 stores means variants to standardize.
  3. pd.to_numeric(s, errors="coerce") and pd.to_numeric(s): when do you use each? Answer: With coerce as a detector (find what is not a number). Without it at the end, as a guarantee: it raises if anything unconvertible is left.

GroupBy Operations

70 min
Objectives (the lesson's own words)
  • Explain split-apply-combine and read a groupby expression piece by piece
  • Summarize groups with built-in methods and with named aggregation, on one key or several
  • Choose correctly between agg (one row per group), transform (one row per original row) and filter (keep or drop whole groups)
  • Control what happens to missing keys and unused categories with dropna= and observed=

Mental model to say

Say: "Imagine the day's receipts from all of Pinecrest dumped on one table. To get revenue by store you would do three things without thinking. Split: deal the receipts into piles, one per store. Apply: add up each pile. Combine: write the totals on one sheet, one line per pile. That is all groupby is, and it has a name: split-apply-combine." Then the question to train all block long: "What shape do I want back? One row per group, the same rows with a group number on each, or fewer of the same rows?" Three mistakes to expect
  • Looking for the key among the columns. Symptom: KeyError: 'store' on a groupby result, or a merge or plot later that cannot find the column. Coach: "Print result.index. Where is store now?" Then let them choose .reset_index() or as_index=False. A cousin: orders.groupby("store").mean() raises TypeError: dtype 'str' does not support operation 'mean' because no column was selected.
  • count when they meant size, and rows lost to a missing key. No error for either. Symptoms: 'orders per payment method' that are ten times too small because ["promo_code"].count() counted only orders with a promo code; group sizes that add up to 2,148 instead of 20,302. Coach: "Do your groups add up to the number of rows in the table? Which column did you count, and does it have gaps?" Make 'do the groups add up?' a spoken check after every groupby today.
  • The average of averages. Symptom: an 'overall average' of 182.66 computed from five store means when the true figure is 194.60. Coach: "How many orders are behind each of those five numbers? Should Asheville count as much as Online?" The cure is to go back to the rows, or to divide a sum of sums by a sum of counts. Expect the same mistake with rates.
