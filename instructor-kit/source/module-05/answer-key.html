2.3 · Cleaning Data: self-check

Q1
An audit shows that the Store column has 31 distinct values. Pinecrest has four stores and an online shop. What is the right first move?
A) Delete the rows whose store name is unusual
B) Print value_counts() to see the variants, then strip, fix the case, and map what is left
C) Convert the column to the category dtype
D) Fill the unusual names with the most common store
Why: Look before you fix. value_counts() shows which variants exist; whitespace and case are mechanical, and the remaining misspellings need a mapping dictionary that you write from what you see.
A is wrong: Those rows are real orders with a badly typed store name. Deleting them deletes revenue.
C is wrong: A category column with 31 categories is still 31 stores. The dtype does not merge spellings.
D is wrong: That would move orders from Boise, Bend and the rest to Online: an invented value, not a repair.
Q2
What does this print?
d = pd.Series(["2024-03-05", "03/05/2024", "March 5, 2024", "2024-02-30"])
iso = pd.to_datetime(d, format="%Y-%m-%d", errors="coerce")
us = pd.to_datetime(d, format="%m/%d/%Y", errors="coerce")
print(iso.notna().sum(), iso.fillna(us).notna().sum())
1 2
Why: The ISO pass understands only the first value: the next two are in other formats and February 30th does not exist, so all three become NaT. Filling from the month/day/year pass recovers the second value. The written-out date needs a third pass, and the impossible date stays missing.
Q3
What does this print?
s = pd.Series(["none", "TRAIL15", "-", "trail15"])
p = s.str.upper()
print(p.where(~p.isin(["NONE", "-"])).tolist())
A) ['NONE', 'TRAIL15', '-', 'TRAIL15']
B) ['TRAIL15', 'TRAIL15']
C) [nan, 'TRAIL15', nan, 'TRAIL15']
D) [nan, 'TRAIL15', nan, 'trail15']
Why: .str.upper() standardizes the case, and .where(condition) keeps a value where the condition is true and puts a missing value where it is false. The two placeholders become real missing values; the length stays 4.
A is wrong: That is p before .where(): the placeholders are still there, counted as promo codes.
B is wrong: .where() never removes rows; it replaces values. The result has four items.
D is wrong: The last code was uppercased before the test, so it is TRAIL15.
Q4
In orders_messy.csv one order total is $4,990.00, while the largest genuine order is $4,893.00. What justified dividing the $4,990.00 by 100?
A) It is above the 99th percentile of order totals
B) It is an online order, and online totals are less reliable
C) It is a round number, which suggests a typing error
D) Its item lines add up to $34.95, so the recorded total is over 100 times what was bought
Why: An outlier is a question, not a verdict. The independent evidence is in order_items.csv: the order's items total $34.95, and the recorded total is about 143 times that. The genuine $4,893 order matches its items.
A is wrong: So is the genuine $4,893 bulk order. Being extreme is not evidence of an error.
B is wrong: Nothing in the data says online totals are less reliable; the twelve largest genuine orders are all online.
C is wrong: Plenty of real totals are round numbers. That is a hunch, not evidence.
Q5
What does this print?
df = pd.DataFrame({"total": [10.0, None, 50.0]})
print(df["total"].mean(), df["total"].fillna(0).mean())
30.0 20.0
Why: .mean() skips the missing value: (10 + 50) / 2 = 30. Filling the gap with 0 claims that the customer paid nothing and divides by three: 60 / 3 = 20. A fill is a claim about the data.
Q6
trial has 406 missing payment methods. What happens when you run trial["payment_method"].fillna("unknown", inplace=True)?
A) trial is unchanged, and pandas warns with ChainedAssignmentError
B) The 406 gaps in trial are filled with unknown
C) A TypeError, because text cannot be used to fill a column
D) The gaps are filled, but only in the first 406 rows
Why: trial["payment_method"] is a new, derived object. Under copy-on-write, filling it 'in place' changes that temporary object and never reaches trial. Assign the result back: trial["payment_method"] = trial["payment_method"].fillna("unknown").
B is wrong: That is what older pandas did, and what old tutorials promise. Under copy-on-write it no longer happens.
C is wrong: Filling a text column with text is fine; the problem is where the result goes.
D is wrong: fillna fills by position of the gaps, not the first rows; and here nothing in trial is filled at all.

2.3 · Predict, then run

P1
Case without whitespace.
s = pd.Series([" Bend", "bend ", "BEND", "Bend OR"])
print(s.str.title().nunique(), s.str.strip().str.title().nunique())
4 2
Why: Title case alone leaves four different values, because two of them still carry a space and Bend OR becomes Bend Or. Stripping first merges the three spellings of Bend. Two remain: Bend and Bend Or, and the second needs a mapping, which is a decision, not a mechanical step.
P2
A detector for values that are not numbers.
s = pd.Series(["$1,200.50", "80", "free", None])
n = pd.to_numeric(s.str.replace("$", "").str.replace(",", ""), errors="coerce")
print(n.tolist(), n.isna().sum())
[1200.5, 80.0, nan, nan] 2
Why: After the symbols are removed, two values convert. free cannot, so coerce makes it NaN; the empty value was already missing. Two NaNs, for two different reasons: one was a word that needs a decision (free means 0), the other was never there.
P3
Three ways to lose rows.
df = pd.DataFrame({"id": [1, 2, 2, 3], "promo": [None, "A", "A", None],
                   "amt": [5.0, 7.0, 7.0, None]})
print(len(df.drop_duplicates()), len(df.dropna()), len(df.dropna(subset=["amt"])))
3 2 3
Why: One exact duplicate goes, leaving 3. dropna() with no arguments drops every row with a gap in any column: only the two rows with a promo code survive. Naming the column that matters keeps the 3 rows that have an amount.

Question: If someone simply added up Order Total in orders_messy.csv, how far off would they be, and which flaw moves the sum the most?

How the solution works: The TOTAL row's 3,950,701.45 is the sum of all true order totals (it equals the sum of order_total in orders.csv). The lab converts the column, takes the careless sum, then applies three repairs in turn and records the sum after each: dropping the 212 duplicates, making the 15 negative totals positive, and dividing the 8 lost-decimal totals by 100 (found with the item-subtotal ratio from the lesson). qs = pd.Series(["12", "7", "n/a", "3.5"])

A column holds " boise", "BOISE " and "Boise". After .str.title() alone, how many distinct values does it have?
A) 1
B) 2
C) 3
Why: Title case does not remove whitespace, so a leading space, a trailing space and no space are still three different texts. Strip first, then fix the case.

Forty of 20,302 orders have no recorded total, and nothing in the file lets you work it out. You need the average order value. What is the most defensible approach?
A) Fill the 40 gaps with 0 so that every row has a number
B) Leave them missing, compute the mean of the known totals, and report that 40 were excluded
C) Call dropna() on the table first, then take the mean
Why: mean() skips missing values, so the average is computed from the 20,262 known totals, and stating that 40 were excluded lets a reader judge the result. No value is invented.

qd = pd.DataFrame({"id": [1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 3], "v": ["a", "a", "b", "c", "c", "d"]})

2.4 · GroupBy Operations: self-check

Q7
What does this print?
df = pd.DataFrame({"store": ["a", "a", "b"], "amt": [10, 30, 5]})
r = df.groupby("store")["amt"].agg(["count", "mean"])
print(r.shape, r.loc["a", "mean"])
A) (3, 2) 20.0
B) (2, 2) 40.0
C) (2,) 20.0
D) (2, 2) 20.0
Why: Two groups give two rows, and two function names give two columns. The index is the store, so r.loc["a", "mean"] is the mean of 10 and 30.
A is wrong: Aggregation returns one row per group, not one per original row.
B is wrong: 40 is the sum for store a; the column asked for is the mean.
C is wrong: A list of two functions makes a DataFrame with two columns, not a Series.
Q8
What does this print?
df = pd.DataFrame({"k": ["x", "x", "y"], "v": [1, 3, 10]})
df["share"] = df["v"] / df.groupby("k")["v"].transform("sum")
print(df["share"].tolist())
[0.25, 0.75, 1.0]
Why: transform("sum") returns one value per original row: 4, 4, 10 (each row's group total). Dividing each value by its own group's total gives each row's share of its group.
Q9
orders has a True/False column returned. Which expression gives the fraction of each channel's orders that were returned?
A) orders.groupby("channel")["returned"].mean()
B) orders.groupby("channel")["returned"].count()
C) orders.groupby("channel")["returned"].size()
D) orders.groupby("channel")["returned"].nunique()
Why: True counts as 1 and False as 0, so the mean of a boolean column within each group is the rate.
B is wrong: count is the number of non-missing values: every order, returned or not.
C is wrong: size is the number of rows in each group.
D is wrong: nunique is the number of distinct values: 2 for each channel (True and False).
Q10
orders.groupby("promo_code").size().sum() gives 2,148, but orders has 20,302 rows. Why?
A) size() skips rows whose order total is missing
B) groupby removes duplicate rows before it counts
C) Rows whose key is missing are dropped by default; dropna=False keeps them as a group
D) promo_code is text and must be a category before grouping
Why: 18,154 orders have no promo code. By default groupby silently leaves out every row whose key is missing. dropna=False adds a group for them, and the sizes then add up to 20,302.
A is wrong: size() counts rows whatever they contain; it does not look at order_total.
B is wrong: groupby never removes duplicates.
D is wrong: Text keys group perfectly well. The dtype is not the issue.
Q11
What does this print?
df = pd.DataFrame({"g": ["a", "a", "b"], "x": [1.0, None, 3.0]})
print(df.groupby("g")["x"].size().tolist(), df.groupby("g")["x"].count().tolist())
[2, 1] [1, 1]
Why: size counts rows: two in group a, one in group b. count counts non-missing values of x: group a has one missing, so 1 and 1.
Q12
You want the original order rows of every customer who placed 25 or more orders. Which call returns them?
A) orders.groupby("customer_id")["order_id"].count() >= 25
B) orders.groupby("customer_id").filter(lambda g: len(g) >= 25)
C) orders.groupby("customer_id").agg(n=("order_id", "count"))
D) orders.groupby("customer_id")["order_id"].transform("count")
Why: filter hands each group to the function and keeps all rows of the groups for which it returns True: fewer of the same rows.
A is wrong: That is a True/False Series with one value per customer, not order rows.
C is wrong: That is a summary with one row per customer.
D is wrong: That is one count per order row: the first half of the transform-then-mask pattern. You would still need orders[... >= 25].

2.4 · Predict, then run

P1
An average of averages.
df = pd.DataFrame({"team": ["a", "b", "a", "b", "a"], "pts": [2, 10, 4, 20, 6]})
means = df.groupby("team")["pts"].mean()
print(means.tolist(), means.mean(), df["pts"].mean())
[4.0, 15.0] 9.5 8.4
Why: Team a averages 4 over three rows and team b averages 15 over two rows. The mean of those two means, 9.5, weights the teams equally; the mean of all five rows, 8.4, weights every row equally. They differ because the groups are different sizes.
P2
A key with gaps.
df = pd.DataFrame({"code": ["X", None, "X", None], "amt": [1, 2, 3, 4]})
print(len(df.groupby("code")), len(df.groupby("code", dropna=False)),
      df.groupby("code")["amt"].sum().sum())
1 2 4
Why: By default the two rows with no code are dropped, so there is one group (X) and the sums add up to 1 + 3 = 4, not 10. With dropna=False the missing key becomes a second group.
P3
Three shapes.
df = pd.DataFrame({"k": ["a", "a", "b"], "v": [1, 2, 3]})
a = df.groupby("k")["v"].sum()
t = df.groupby("k")["v"].transform("sum")
f = df.groupby("k").filter(lambda g: len(g) > 1)
print(len(a), len(t), len(f))
2 3 2
Why: agg (here sum) returns one row per group: 2. transform returns one value per original row: 3. filter returns the original rows of the groups that pass the test: only group a has more than one row, so 2 rows.

2.4 Q1
What does this print?
qdf = pd.DataFrame({"team": ["a", "a", "b", "b", "b"], "pts": [1, 3, 2, 4, 6]})
qres = qdf.groupby("team")["pts"].transform("mean")
print(len(qres), qres.tolist())
A) 2 [2.0, 4.0]
B) 5 [2.0, 2.0, 4.0, 4.0, 4.0]
C) 5 [1.0, 3.0, 2.0, 4.0, 6.0]
Why: transform returns one value for every original row: each row gets the mean of its own group. Team a averages 2 and team b averages 4, repeated for their two and three rows.
2.4 Q2
You want one row per store, with a column orders (the number of orders) and a column revenue (the sum of order_total). Which call produces it?
A) orders.groupby("store").agg({"order_total": {"revenue": "sum"}})
B) orders.groupby("store")["order_total"].transform("sum")
C) orders.groupby("store").agg(orders=("order_id", "count"), revenue=("order_total", "sum"))
Why: Named aggregation: each keyword is the new column name, and its tuple gives the source column and the function. The result has one row per store and exactly these two columns.
2.4 Q3
Two of the five rows have no code. What does this print?
qd2 = pd.DataFrame({"code": ["X", None, "X", None, "Y"], "amt": [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]})
print(qd2.groupby("code")["amt"].sum().sum())
A) 9
B) 15
C) nan
Why: By default groupby drops rows whose key is missing. Only X (1 + 3) and Y (5) form groups, so the group sums total 9, not 15. dropna=False would keep the missing key as a group.
2.4 Q4
The five stores' average order totals are $174.90, $172.91, $177.22, $183.61 and $204.65. Their mean is $182.66, but the average over all orders is $194.60. Why do the two differ?
A) Rounding errors build up when averages are averaged
B) Missing order totals were skipped in one calculation and not in the other
C) The groups are very different sizes, and a mean of means weights each group equally
Why: Averaging the five store averages gives each store equal weight, but the online shop has 12,854 orders and Asheville 1,413. The true overall average weights every order equally, so the large online group pulls it up.
2.4 Q5
Which statement about orders.groupby("payment_method")["promo_code"] is true?
A) .size() counts all orders in each group; .count() counts only those with a promo code
B) .size() and .count() always return the same numbers
C) .count() returns the number of distinct promo codes in each group
Why: size counts the rows in each group whatever they contain. count counts only the non-missing values of the selected column, so it reports how many orders in each group used a promo code.

Warm-up Answers

  1. What three things make up a Series? Answer: An array of values, an index of labels, and an optional name.
  2. On a table with the default index, how many rows do df.loc[0:3] and df.iloc[0:3] return? Answer: 4 and 3: a label slice includes its end, a position slice leaves out the stop.
  3. df[df["price"] > 100]["price"] = 0 changes nothing. What do you write instead? Answer: df.loc[df["price"] > 100, "price"] = 0: one .loc on the table you want to change.
  4. A column of birth years loads as float64 and prints 1989.0. Why, and what is the fix? Answer: Some years are missing and NumPy's int64 cannot hold a missing value. Load it as "Int64" (capital I).
  5. After pd.read_excel(...) the columns are called Unnamed: 1, Unnamed: 2. What happened, and what do you check after every load? Answer: The header is not on the row pandas read as the header (skiprows / header). After every load: shape, dtypes, head().

Exit Ticket Answers

  1. In what order do you repair a damaged table, and why does structure come first? Answer: Structure (headers, rows that are not data, duplicates), then text, numbers, dates, missing values. Fixing the shape first makes later steps simpler and counts honest (for example astype fails while a TOTAL row is present).
  2. Forty of 20,302 order totals are blank and nothing in the file gives their value. You need the average order value. What do you do, and what do you say? Answer: Leave them missing, take the mean of the known totals, and report that 40 orders were excluded. Filling with 0 or with the median would invent data.
  3. One row per group; one value per original row; fewer of the same rows. Name the groupby tool for each. Answer: agg (or a built-in such as sum); transform; filter (or a transform and a mask).

Common Wrong Answers and What They Reveal

  • Q2 answered 2 2 or 1 3. They expect one format string to understand another format, or they expect the impossible date to be parsed. Have them print iso and us side by side: each pass understands its own format only, and February 30th stays NaT.
  • Q5 answered 30.0 30.0. They think fillna(0) is neutral. Ask what three customers paid in the second calculation: 10, 0 and 50.
  • Q6 answered B ('the gaps are filled'). The copy-on-write rule from Session 4 has not transferred to inplace=True. Show the cell and the warning; the fix is to assign the result back.
  • Q4 answered A ('above the 99th percentile'). They treat 'extreme' as 'wrong'. Remind them that the $4,893 bulk order is extreme and true.
  • Q8 answered [4, 4, 10] or a two-item list. They are thinking of agg. Draw the two shapes: transform returns one value per original row.
  • Q11 answered [2, 1] [2, 1]. size and count are the same to them. Ask what count does with the missing value in group a.
  • Exit ticket 2 answered 'fill with the mean'. It leaves the mean unchanged, which feels safe, but it invents 40 perfectly average orders and shrinks the spread. With 40 gaps in 20,302 rows, leaving them missing costs nothing.
  • Lab register_log: wrong: 1. The lost decimal was missed, or a threshold on revenue caught the genuine promo day. Ask for the evidence in the row (revenue per transaction).
  • Lab store_days: 726 days for Asheville. count on revenue was used for the number of days. Ask what happens to a day with no revenue figure.
