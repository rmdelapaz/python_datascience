Session 7: Time Series and Windows

How to teach the session where the calendar becomes part of the data. Before the break, learners turn a text column into a ruler: they select by date string, find the 21 missing store-days in daily_sales.csv, decide which are true zeros and which are unknown, and roll days up into weeks, months and years without letting a gap pass as a bad month. After the break they slide windows along that ruler: moving averages, running totals, ranks, and the one idea that matters most, a baseline that never contains the day it judges.

The risk of Session 7: the worst mistakes are silent

Almost nothing in this session raises an error when it goes wrong. A sum over a month with three missing days returns a number. diff(7) on a table with missing rows compares a Saturday with a Wednesday and returns a number. A rolling mean over a long table averages four different stores and returns a number. A baseline that contains the day it judges returns a number. Train two habits all session and write them on the board: "Count beside every sum" and "On that day, could I have known every number in it?"

Known environment traps

Old frequency aliases. Anyone who copies from a search result or an older book will type resample("M"). pandas 3.0 raises ValueError: Invalid frequency: M ... Please use 'ME' instead. The demo triggers it on purpose (2.7 step 10) so the room has seen it.

A different pandas. Run pd.__version__ on a learner machine that prints a FutureWarning instead of an error for "M", or shows datetime64[ns] where the guide shows datetime64[us]: that machine has pandas 2.x. The code still works; send them to the setup guide after class.

Charts in a script. If someone works in a .py file and not a notebook, plt.show() opens a window that blocks the script until it is closed. Tell them to close the window. (The kit labs never call plt.show().)

The working folder. FileNotFoundError: data/daily_sales.csv means the notebook is not in the course folder. The kit labs do not have this problem: they find the data through labs_paths.

Warm-up: Retrieval from Session 6

10 min
How to run it

Closed notebooks, closed laptops. Read each question aloud, give 30 seconds of silent writing, then take one answer from the room before giving yours. Five questions in six minutes; do not re-teach. A question that most of the room misses becomes a two-minute recap at the start of the 2.7 demo, where the same idea returns (question 4 returns in step 4, when you pivot to the wide table).

Five retrieval questions (with answers)
  1. What is the default how= of merge, and what happens to a row whose key has no match? Answer: inner. The row is dropped with no warning. In Lesson 2.5 that lost 739 orders whose customer has no row in customers.csv. Use how="left" when you are adding columns to a main table.
  2. After a merge the result has more rows than the left table. What does that tell you? Answer: The key repeats on the other side (one-to-many or many-to-many), so left rows were duplicated. Count rows before and after, and state the relationship with validate=.
  3. What does pivot_table compute in each cell if you leave out aggfunc? Answer: The mean. A table titled "revenue" built that way shows average order values. Always write aggfunc.
  4. df.pivot(index="date", columns="store", values="revenue") raises ValueError: Index contains duplicate entries, cannot reshape. What does the error tell you about the data? Answer: At least one date and store pair appears on more than one row. pivot only rearranges; choose how to combine the repeats and use pivot_table(..., aggfunc=...).
  5. A sheet has one row per month and one column per store. Which method turns it into tidy rows of month, store, revenue? Answer: melt(id_vars="Month", var_name="store", value_name="revenue"). Twelve rows and five store columns become 60 rows.
Homework check

Lesson 2.6 asked learners to rebuild wide (daily revenue, one column per store) from memory and to look at wide.loc["2024-08-10":"2024-08-16", "Bend"]. Ask: "Who looked at Bend in August? What did you see?" Take the answer (three NaN in a row) and park it: "Hold on to that. In twenty minutes you will know what to do about it, and what not to do." If fewer than half the room did the homework, say so plainly and move on; the demo rebuilds wide in step 4.

The mental model to say

Say: "To a computer, the text 2024-03-01 is ten characters, no more related to 2024-02-29 than 'cat' is to 'dog'. A datetime is a point on a ruler. Points on a ruler have an order, distances between them, and regular marks (days, weeks, months) you can group by. Everything today is something you can do with a ruler and cannot do with labels."

Then ask the three questions the lesson opens with and leave them unanswered: Is Asheville's revenue falling? Does Flagstaff grow all year or only in summer? What did the sale weekend add? Say: "Each of those is about order in time. By the break you will have answered the first two."

Point at the board and read the order of operations once: parse, index, complete the calendar, decide each gap, then compute. Say: "Most time series mistakes come from skipping the third step. Today you will watch that happen twice."

Three mistakes learners will make, and how to coach each
  • Resampling or using .dt on text, or on a table with no date index. Symptoms: AttributeError: Can only use .dt accessor with datetimelike values, or TypeError: Only valid with DatetimeIndex, TimedeltaIndex or PeriodIndex, but got an instance of 'RangeIndex'. Coach with a question, not the fix: "What does df.dtypes say? What is the index right now?" Then: parse_dates at load, and set_index/pivot or resample("ME", on="date").
  • Trusting a total without its count. There is no symptom: the learner reports "Bend's best week" or "August grew" from a bucket with missing days. Coach: "How many days are behind that number?" Make them add count to the agg list and read the row again. In the lab this is the 4-day week ending 2024-08-18.
  • Filling every gap the same way. Learners write wide.fillna(0) (which turns five failed exports and six blank values into days with no sales) or wide.interpolate() (which invents sales on Christmas). Coach: "Was the store open that day? How do you know?" Send them to the gap list in data/README.md. Closed is 0; unknown stays NaN unless you estimate and say so.

Window Functions

75 min in the room
Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Explain how a window calculation differs from a groupby summary, and choose between rolling, expanding and exponentially weighted windows
  • Smooth a series with rolling(), and control the edges and gaps with min_periods, time-based windows and center=True
  • Build running totals and year-to-date figures with cumsum() and expanding()
  • Apply windows and ranks within groups, and attach the result to the original table without misaligning it
  • Recognize look-ahead leakage and write a window that uses only the past

The mental model to say

Say: "Before the break, monthly totals smoothed the daily zigzag, but at a price: 731 days became 24 numbers. A window function gives you both. It computes a summary for every row, from a small set of nearby rows. The table keeps its length; each row gains some context."

Say: "From a train you never see the whole route. You see what fits in the window, and the view changes a little every meter. A rolling window is that pane of glass: always the last 7 days. An expanding window is a camera that started filming at the first station and never stops: everything so far. An exponentially weighted window is your memory of the trip: the last minutes are vivid, the early stations fade."

Draw two boxes on the board: groupby().agg() → one row per group; window → one row per input row. Ask: "Which tool from Session 5 also returned one value per row?" (transform.) That is the bridge to step 9.

Three mistakes learners will make, and how to coach each
  • A window that runs across groups. On a long table, df["revenue"].rolling(7).mean() averages different stores together and raises nothing. Coach with the quick test: "Are the first rows of each store NaN, or only the top of the table?" Then: sort by group and date, groupby(...).transform(lambda s: s.rolling(7).mean()). A related error message, when they assign groupby(...).rolling(...) straight back: TypeError: incompatible index of inserted column with frame index.
  • min_periods=1 to make the NaN go away. The numbers appear, and the first "28-day average" is one day. Coach: "How many days is the value on row 3 built from? Would you show that to a manager as a monthly average?" Agree on a threshold they can defend: 6 of 7, 21 of 28.
  • A baseline that includes the row it judges. rev / rev.rolling(7).mean() and anything with center=True. No symptom except results that look too good. Coach with the board question: "On that day, could you have known every number in the window?" Fix: shift(1) before the window.

Exit ticket (3 questions, with answers)
  1. You resample daily revenue to monthly totals with .sum(). What do you print beside every total, and why? Answer: The count of days with a value (for example .agg(["sum", "count"])). Sums skip NaN silently, so a month with missing days looks like a weak month.
