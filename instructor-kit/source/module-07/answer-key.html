2.7 · Time Series: self-check

Q1
After raw = pd.read_csv("data/daily_sales.csv"), the line raw["date"].dt.month raises AttributeError: Can only use .dt accessor with datetimelike values. What is the cause?
A) Some dates are missing from the file
B) The column was loaded as text; pass parse_dates=["date"] or convert with pd.to_datetime
C) .dt only works on the index, not on a column
D) month is a method and needs parentheses
Why: With no instructions read_csv keeps dates as text, and text has no .dt accessor. Parse at load time, then check df.dtypes.
A is wrong: Missing days do not change the dtype of the days that are present.
C is wrong: .dt is for columns (Series). A DatetimeIndex has the same properties directly, without .dt.
D is wrong: .dt.month is a property. The error is raised at .dt, before month is even looked up.
Q2
What does this print?
s = pd.Series(range(10),
              index=pd.date_range("2025-01-28", periods=10, freq="D"))
print(len(s.loc["2025-01"]), len(s.loc["2025-01-30":"2025-02-02"]))
4 4
Why: "2025-01" selects every row in January: the 28th, 29th, 30th and 31st. The string range includes both ends: January 30 and 31, February 1 and 2.
Q3
wide has one row per date and 727 rows between 2024-01-01 and 2025-12-31, a span of 731 days. What does wide.asfreq("D") return?
A) 727 rows; the four gaps are interpolated
B) 731 rows; the four new rows are filled with 0
C) An error, because the index has missing dates
D) 731 rows; the four new rows hold NaN
Why: asfreq("D") gives the table one row for every calendar day. Rows that did not exist appear as NaN: pandas shows you the gap and leaves the decision to you.
A is wrong: pandas never estimates values unless you call interpolate() or ffill() yourself.
B is wrong: Zero is a claim (the store sold nothing). pandas does not make it for you; you fill closed days with 0 on purpose.
C is wrong: Missing dates are exactly what asfreq is for. It needs a DatetimeIndex, not a complete one.
Q4
What does this print?
s = pd.Series([10.0, np.nan, 30.0],
              index=pd.to_datetime(["2025-05-01", "2025-05-02", "2025-05-03"]))
print(s.resample("ME").agg(["sum", "count"]).values.tolist())
A) [[40.0, 2.0]]
B) [[nan, 3.0]]
C) [[40.0, 3.0]]
D) [[nan, 2.0]]
Why: sum skips the missing value and adds 10 + 30. count counts only real values, so it says 2, which is how you find out that the total of 40 is built from two days and not three.
B is wrong: pandas aggregations skip NaN by default, and count never counts a missing value.
C is wrong: count is the number of non-missing values, not the number of rows in the bucket.
D is wrong: The count is right, but the sum is not NaN: sum skips missing values (that is the danger).
Q5
Bend has no rows at all for three days in August 2024. On that rows-only Series, what does bend_rows.diff(7) compare the first Saturday after the gap with?
A) The previous Saturday, seven days earlier
B) Nothing: the result is NaN because the window crosses a gap
C) The value seven rows earlier, which is ten days back and not a Saturday
D) Nothing: pandas raises an error on an irregular index
Why: shift and diff count rows, not days. With three rows missing, seven rows back is ten days back. No error and no warning: complete the calendar with asfreq("D") first.
A is wrong: That is what you want, and what you get only on a complete calendar.
B is wrong: NaN is what the completed calendar gives for the days after a gap, because a week earlier is unknown there. Rows-only data has no NaN to pass on.
D is wrong: pandas is happy to shift any index. The mistake is silent.
Q6
What does this print?
m = pd.Series([200, 220, 264],
              index=pd.date_range("2025-01-31", periods=3, freq="ME"))
print((m.pct_change() * 100).round(1).tolist())
[nan, 10.0, 20.0]
Why: pct_change() is the change from the previous row divided by the previous row: 20/200 = 10% and 44/220 = 20%. January has no previous row, so it is NaN.

s = pd.Series([5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 9, 9, 9, 9, 9, 9, 9],

Why: March 3, 2025 is a Monday, and the first Sunday (March 9) was deleted. The first week has six rows of 5 (sum 30, count 6); the second has seven rows of 9 (sum 63, count 7). The sum alone would suggest the second week was twice as good; per recorded day it went from 5 to 9.
Predict 2.7.3
What does diff() print for the last row, and why is that surprising?
s = pd.Series([100, 104, 120],
              index=pd.to_datetime(["2025-06-01", "2025-06-02", "2025-06-06"]))
print(s.diff().tolist())
print(s.asfreq("D").diff().tolist())
[nan, 4.0, 16.0]
[nan, 4.0, nan, nan, nan, nan]
Why: On the rows-only Series, diff() subtracts the previous row: 120 - 104 = 16, although that row is four days earlier. After asfreq("D") the three missing days exist as NaN, and every difference that touches one of them is NaN: the honest answer to "how much did it change since yesterday?"

What it practices: the order of operations on a new column (units) and a new bucket (weeks). units has no blank values, so after zeroing the four closed days the only unknowns are the five failed-export days (Bend 3, Flagstaff 2).

2.8 · Window Functions: self-check

Q7
What does this print?
s = pd.Series([4, 8, 6, 10])
print(s.rolling(3, min_periods=2).mean().tolist())
A) [nan, nan, 6.0, 8.0]
B) [4.0, 6.0, 6.0, 8.0]
C) [nan, 6.0, 6.0, 8.0]
D) [6.0, 6.0, 8.0, nan]
Why: The window is this row and the two before it, and it answers as soon as 2 values are present. Row 1 has only one value (NaN); row 2 is the mean of 4 and 8; then the full windows give 6 and 8.
A is wrong: That is the strict default, where all 3 values must be present.
B is wrong: That is min_periods=1, where one value is enough.
D is wrong: A trailing window looks backward, so the gap is at the start, not the end.
Q8
What does this print?
s = pd.Series([5, 3, 9, 4, 9])
print((s == s.cummax()).tolist())
[True, False, True, False, True]
Why: cummax() is the best value so far: 5, 5, 9, 9, 9. A row equals it when it sets or ties the record. The last 9 ties the record, so it is True as well.
Q9
A long table has columns store, date, revenue, sorted by date and then store. Which code adds each store's own 7-day average, lined up with the right rows?
A) df["avg"] = df["revenue"].rolling(7).mean()
B) df["avg"] = df.groupby("store")["revenue"].rolling(7).mean()
C) df["avg"] = df.groupby("date")["revenue"].transform(lambda s: s.rolling(7).mean())
D) Sort by store and date, then df["avg"] = df.groupby("store")["revenue"].transform(lambda s: s.rolling(7).mean())
Why: Sort, group, window. transform returns one value per row with the original index, so the new column lines up.
A is wrong: Seven consecutive rows are different stores on neighboring days: a number with no meaning.
B is wrong: The result has a two-level index (store, row label), and assigning it back raises TypeError: incompatible index of inserted column with frame index.
C is wrong: Grouping by date puts four stores of one day in each group: every result is NaN, and it is the wrong grouping anyway.
Q10
What does this print?
s = pd.Series([30, 10, 30, 20])
print(s.rank(ascending=False, method="dense").tolist())
A) [1.0, 3.0, 1.0, 2.0]
B) [1.5, 4.0, 1.5, 3.0]
C) [1.0, 4.0, 1.0, 3.0]
D) [3.0, 1.0, 3.0, 2.0]
Why: With ascending=False the largest value gets rank 1. The two 30s tie for first. "dense" leaves no gap after a tie, so 20 is rank 2 and 10 is rank 3.
B is wrong: That is the default method="average": the tied values share positions 1 and 2.
C is wrong: That is method="min" (sports ranking): after two firsts the next rank is 3.
D is wrong: That is a dense rank with the default ascending=True, where the smallest value is rank 1.
Q11
To flag unusual days, an analyst computes lift = rev / rev.rolling(28).mean() and flags days where lift >= 1.5. What is wrong with the baseline?
A) A 28-day window is too long to react to a sale
B) Each day is inside its own baseline, so every spike is understated; use rev.shift(1).rolling(28).mean()
C) A rolling mean cannot be used as a divisor
D) The first 27 rows are NaN, so the division fails
Why: The window ends on the day being judged, so part of the spike is already in the average it is compared with. Shift first, then roll: the baseline then holds only what was known that morning.
A is wrong: Window length is a judgment call, not an error. The leak is the problem at any length.
C is wrong: Dividing two aligned Series is ordinary pandas.
D is wrong: NaN rows give NaN lifts and are simply not flagged; nothing fails.
Q12
What does this print?
s = pd.Series([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
print(s.shift(1).rolling(2).sum().tolist())
[nan, nan, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0]
Why: shift(1) gives NaN, 1, 2, 3, 4. A 2-row sum needs two real values: the first two rows have none or one, then 1+2, 2+3, 3+4. Each result uses only the rows before its own.

Predict 2.8.2
Two groups stacked in one column. What does each line print?
t = pd.DataFrame({"g": ["a", "a", "a", "b", "b", "b"],
                  "v": [1, 2, 3, 10, 20, 30]})
print(t["v"].cumsum().tolist())
print(t.groupby("g")["v"].cumsum().tolist())
[1, 3, 6, 16, 36, 66]
[1, 3, 6, 10, 30, 60]
Why: The plain cumsum runs straight through both groups: 6 + 10 = 16 carries group a's total into group b. The grouped version restarts at the first row of each group. This is the long-table trap in its smallest form, and it is how year to date works.
Predict 2.8.3
Which of the three columns could you have computed on the morning of each row? Predict the last row of each.
s = pd.Series([10, 10, 10, 40, 10])
print(s.rolling(3).mean().round(1).tolist())
print(s.rolling(3, center=True).mean().round(1).tolist())

Why: Only the third. The trailing mean already contains the 40 on the day of the spike (20.0). The centered mean sees the spike one row early (20.0 on the third row) and has no value on the last row. The shifted version is still 10.0 on the day of the spike and only reacts the day after.

Lab 2.8-B · The online shop: milestones, ranks and a fair baseline: labs/module-07/l2_8-window-functions/online_milestones_solution.py

What it practices: a running total that restarts per group, ranking within groups, cummax(), and the leakage idea from the project on a new series.

Step 1. 2024 passed $1,000,000 on December 11; 2025 on October 16, about eight weeks earlier in the year. Full-year totals: 1,077,800.75 and 1,280,183.70 (+18.8%).

Step 2. December is rank 1 and February rank 12 in both years. In between, the order changes: May was 2nd in 2024 and 5th in 2025. rank returns floats; the solution converts to int only because there are no ties.

Step 4. The record day, 2025-06-24, is a Tuesday with $8,960.15. Against the mean of the four Tuesdays before it (4,066.72) it is 2.20 times normal. With the day inside its own 4-Tuesday window the "baseline" rises to 5,583.56 and the ratio falls to 1.60. Across two years, 25 days reach 2 times an honest baseline and only 2 reach 2 times a leaky one: the leak hides exactly the days the baseline exists to find.

A question worth asking: why was that Tuesday so large? done[done["order_date"] == "2025-06-24"] shows one order of $4,184.90, one of the twelve bulk outfitter orders described in data/README.md. A real order, not an error.

Warm-up Answers

  1. What is the default how= of merge, and what happens to a row whose key has no match? Answer: inner. The row is dropped with no warning. In Lesson 2.5 that lost 739 orders whose customer has no row in customers.csv. Use how="left" when you are adding columns to a main table.
  2. After a merge the result has more rows than the left table. What does that tell you? Answer: The key repeats on the other side (one-to-many or many-to-many), so left rows were duplicated. Count rows before and after, and state the relationship with validate=.
  3. What does pivot_table compute in each cell if you leave out aggfunc? Answer: The mean. A table titled "revenue" built that way shows average order values. Always write aggfunc.
  4. df.pivot(index="date", columns="store", values="revenue") raises ValueError: Index contains duplicate entries, cannot reshape. What does the error tell you about the data? Answer: At least one date and store pair appears on more than one row. pivot only rearranges; choose how to combine the repeats and use pivot_table(..., aggfunc=...).
  5. A sheet has one row per month and one column per store. Which method turns it into tidy rows of month, store, revenue? Answer: melt(id_vars="Month", var_name="store", value_name="revenue"). Twelve rows and five store columns become 60 rows.

Exit Ticket Answers

  1. You resample daily revenue to monthly totals with .sum(). What do you print beside every total, and why? Answer: The count of days with a value (for example .agg(["sum", "count"])). Sums skip NaN silently, so a month with missing days looks like a weak month.
  2. Write an expression for a 7-day average of the Series rev that is safe to use for judging or predicting today. Answer: rev.shift(1).rolling(7).mean() (or rev.rolling(7, closed="left").mean()). Shift first, so today is not inside its own baseline.
  3. List the order of operations for a time series, and name the step people skip most often. Answer: Parse the dates; put them in the index; complete the calendar with asfreq; decide what each gap means; only then resample, shift or compare. The skipped step is completing the calendar.

Reading the tickets: a ticket that says "mean" or "nothing" for question 1 needs the Bend August example again. For question 2, rev.rolling(7).mean().shift(1) is also correct (the same numbers); rev.rolling(7).mean() and anything with center=True are the leak, and those learners get a two-minute recap at the start of Session 8, where leakage returns as the cutoff rule. For question 3, accept any wording that puts the calendar before the arithmetic.
