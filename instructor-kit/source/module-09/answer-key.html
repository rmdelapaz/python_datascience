3.2 · Subplots and Composition: self-check

Q1
What does this print?
fig, axs = plt.subplots(2, 3)
print(axs.shape, len(fig.axes), type(axs[1, 2]).__name__)
plt.close(fig)
(2, 3) 6 Axes
Why: Rows first, then columns: a 2 by 3 NumPy array holding six Axes. axs[1, 2] is row 1, column 2, the bottom-right panel, and it is one Axes.
Q2
After fig, axs = plt.subplots(1, 4), the line axs[0, 2].set_title("Boise") fails. Why?
A) A grid must have at least two rows before panels can be titled
B) A one-row grid comes back as a 1-D array, so it takes one index: axs[2]
C) set_title only works on the figure: fig.set_title
D) The panels are numbered from 1, so the third panel is axs[1, 3]
Why: plt.subplots squeezes away dimensions of length one. One row of four is an array of shape (4,), and two indexes raise IndexError: too many indices for array. Use squeeze=False when you want two indexes every time.
A is wrong: Any grid can be titled; the problem is the index, not the shape.
C is wrong: A Figure has suptitle; each panel's title is still ax.set_title.
D is wrong: Python indexes from 0; the third panel is position 2.
Q3
Four panels show monthly revenue for four stores. When should you pass sharey=True?
A) Always: panels without a shared scale are wrong
B) Never: sharing squashes the smaller stores
C) When the reader should compare sizes across panels; separate scales show each shape but hide the differences in size
D) Only when the panels are in one row
Why: Sharing is a decision about the question. A shared y axis answers "which is bigger?"; separate axes answer "what shape does each have?". When panels do not share a scale, say so in the title or caption.
A is wrong: Separate scales are right when shape is the point, as long as the reader is told.
B is wrong: Squashing is a cost worth paying when size is the point.
D is wrong: Sharing works for any grid shape, and sharey="row" shares by row.
Q4
What does this print?
fig, axd = plt.subplot_mosaic("AAB;CCB")
print(sorted(axd), len(fig.axes))
plt.close(fig)
['A', 'B', 'C'] 3
Why: The string is a picture: one letter per cell, ; between rows. A spans two cells of the top row, C two cells of the bottom row, and B the right column of both rows. Three names, three panels, returned as a dictionary.
Q5
Which chart is an honest use of a second y axis?
A) Revenue on the left and visitors on the right, made with twinx()
B) Revenue on the left and staff on duty on the right
C) Boise's revenue on the left and Asheville's revenue on a separate right-hand scale
D) Temperature in °F on the left and the same values in °C on the right, with secondary_yaxis
Why: When both sides measure the same quantity, a formula ties the two scales together and nobody gets to choose where the lines sit. With two different measures, the scales are arbitrary and the picture can be made to say anything.
A is wrong: Two measures, two arbitrary scales: their crossings and overlaps mean nothing. Stack the panels or plot a ratio.
B is wrong: The same problem, with a causal story ready to be read into it (Lesson 4.4 takes this pair apart).
C is wrong: Two scales for the same unit hide the fact that one store is nearly twice the other.
Q6
Seven categories are drawn on a 3 by 3 grid with for ax, name in zip(axs.flat, categories):. What does the reader see in the last two cells?
A) Two empty frames with ticks from 0 to 1, until you remove them with ax.remove()
B) Nothing: zip deletes the unused panels
C) Copies of the last category
D) An IndexError, because the grid has more panels than names
Why: zip stops at the shorter input and quietly leaves the extra Axes untouched. They look like charts that failed to load. Remove them: for ax in axs.flat[len(categories):]: ax.remove().
B is wrong: zip only stops looping; it does not touch the figure.
C is wrong: Nothing is drawn on the leftover panels at all.
D is wrong: zip never raises for unequal lengths.

Predict 3.2.1
Three grids. What type or shape does each give back?
a = plt.subplots(1, 1)[1]
b = plt.subplots(2, 1)[1]
c = plt.subplots(2, 1, squeeze=False)[1]
print(type(a).__name__, b.shape, c.shape)
plt.close("all")
Axes (2,) (2, 1)
Why: A 1 by 1 grid is squeezed to a single Axes. One column of two is squeezed to a 1-D array of shape (2,). squeeze=False keeps the full 2-D shape (2, 1).
Predict 3.2.2
Two panels with very different data and sharey=True. Same y range or not?
fig, axs = plt.subplots(1, 2, sharey=True)
axs[0].plot([0, 1], [0, 10])
axs[1].plot([0, 1], [0, 2])
print(axs[0].get_ylim() == axs[1].get_ylim())
plt.close(fig)
True
Why: With a shared y axis both panels use one range that fits all the data, here the range of the first panel. The second line now sits low in its panel, which is the honest picture: it is the smaller one.
Predict 3.2.3
A period in the layout picture. How many panels, with which names?
fig, axd = plt.subplot_mosaic([["main", "side"],
                               ["main", "."]])
print(list(axd), len(fig.axes))
plt.close(fig)
['main', 'side'] 2
Why: "main" covers both rows of the left column, so it is one tall panel. "." leaves its cell empty: no panel is made for it. Two names, two Axes.

3.3 · Customizing Plots: self-check

Q7
A chart shows the monthly online return rate for 2024 and 2025; both lines wobble between about 5.7% and 9.1%. Which title serves a reader best?
A) Online return rate by month
B) Online returns are rising
C) The online return rate held near 7% in both years
D) Why customers send things back
Why: It states what the chart shows, in words a reader can check against the lines. What is plotted (monthly rate, online orders, 2024 to 2025) goes in the subtitle.
A is wrong: A description, not a finding: it spends the most-read text on the chart without saying anything.
B is wrong: A claim the data does not support (the yearly rate went from 7.11% to 6.76%). A finding title can be wrong; check it with a number.
D is wrong: The chart shows how often, not why. Causes stay out of a title unless they have been tested.
Q8
What does this print?
x = 1234567
print(f"${x / 1000:,.0f}K", "{x:.1%}".format(x=0.0712))
$1,235K 7.1%
Why: The first divides by 1,000, rounds to no decimals and adds thousands separators: the FuncFormatter recipe for thousands of dollars. The second multiplies by 100 and adds the percent sign. Matplotlib fills the field named x with each tick value.
Q9
A heatmap shows each store's revenue change against the same month last year, from -12.6% to +29.0%. Which color choice fits?
A) A qualitative palette such as Okabe-Ito, one color per cell
B) A sequential map such as viridis, from the lowest change to the highest
C) A diverging map such as RdBu, with the limits set symmetric around zero
D) A rainbow map such as jet, to show as many levels as possible
Why: Zero is a meaningful midpoint: shrinking and growing are different stories. A diverging map gives the two sides different hues and puts the neutral color at zero, which needs vmin=-limit, vmax=limit.
A is wrong: Qualitative colors are for unordered groups; these numbers have an order.
B is wrong: Viridis would color the small positive changes much like the negative ones, so the reader cannot see where zero is.
D is wrong: Jet's brightness rises and falls, inventing boundaries that are not in the data, and it fails color-blind readers.
Q10
A line chart has four series whose ends are well apart. Why replace the legend with direct labels?
A) The reader no longer has to look a color up in a box, and the chart works even when colors are hard to tell apart
B) Matplotlib raises a warning when a legend has more than three entries
C) Direct labels make the figure render faster
D) Legends cannot be used with gray lines
Why: A legend is a lookup table: find a line, note its color, find it in the box, read the name, come back. A label at the end of each line removes the trip, and it does not depend on color at all.
B is wrong: There is no such warning. Legends are fine when lines cannot be labeled at their ends.
C is wrong: Speed has nothing to do with it; this is about the reader.
D is wrong: A legend works with any colors; with several gray lines it just becomes useless, which is the reason to label directly.
Q11
What does this print?
before = plt.rcParams["axes.spines.top"]
with plt.rc_context({"axes.spines.top": False}):
    fig, ax = plt.subplots()
inside = ax.spines["top"].get_visible()
plt.close(fig)
print(before, inside, plt.rcParams["axes.spines.top"])
True False True
Why: The figure was created inside the with block, so it was built with the style: no top spine. When the block ends the setting goes back to the default, True, for everything drawn afterward.
Q12
Which edit makes a bar chart of 2025 store revenue misleading?
A) Removing the top and right spines
B) Starting the y axis at $1.5 million to "show the differences better"
C) Adding a faint horizontal grid behind the bars
D) Writing each bar's value on it with bar_label
Why: A bar's length is its value. With the axis cut at $1.5 million, Boise's bar is drawn about four and a half times as tall as Asheville's, although Boise sells less than twice as much. Bars start at zero.
A is wrong: Removing ink that carries no information is clean-up, not distortion.
C is wrong: A faint grid helps the reader estimate values and stays out of the way.
D is wrong: Direct value labels add information and remove a trip to the axis.

Predict 3.3.1
Two format strings. What does each produce?
print("{x:,.0f}".format(x=1234.6), "${x:,.2f}".format(x=7))
1,235 $7.00
Why: , adds thousands separators and .0f rounds to no decimals: 1,235. .2f always shows two decimals, so 7 becomes $7.00. Matplotlib does exactly this with each tick value.
Predict 3.3.2
Which sides of the frame are still visible?
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
print([side for side in ax.spines if ax.spines[side].get_visible()])
plt.close(fig)
['left', 'bottom']
Why: An Axes has four spines: left, right, bottom, top. Hiding two leaves the left and bottom ones, the usual clean frame.
Predict 3.3.3
Three positions and three labels. What do the tick labels say?
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot([0, 1], [0, 1])
ax.set_xticks([0, 0.5, 1], labels=["start", "middle", "end"])
print([t.get_text() for t in ax.get_xticklabels()])
plt.close(fig)
['start', 'middle', 'end']
Why: set_xticks with labels= fixes the positions and the text together, one label per position. With only two labels for three positions it would raise a ValueError instead.

Warm-up Answers

  1. Before you build a feature table, what is the first thing you decide? Answer: The unit of analysis: what one row represents. Every feature must be one fact about that unit.
  2. After a left merge of per-customer summaries, which NaN values become 0 and which stay missing? Answer: Counts and sums become 0 (true statements). Averages, shares and recency stay missing; add a flag such as has_ordered.
  3. State the cutoff rule. Answer: For every feature: could I have computed this value on the cutoff date, using only what existed then? If not, it leaks.
  4. What is the difference between a Figure and an Axes? Answer: The Figure is the whole canvas, the thing that gets saved. An Axes is one chart on it, with its own x and y axis.
  5. ax.bar(daily["store"], daily["revenue"]) draws four bars and no error. What are their heights? Answer: Each store's single highest day: 2,903 bars pile up at four positions and the tallest shows. ax.bar never aggregates; group first.

Exit Ticket Answers

  1. What does plt.subplots(2, 2) give you in axs, and what is the safest way to loop over the panels? Answer: A 2-D NumPy array of four Axes. Loop with for ax, item in zip(axs.flat, items):, which works for every grid shape.
  2. Why is a twin-axis chart of two different measures risky, and what do you draw instead? Answer: The two scales are arbitrary, so where the lines sit, cross or overlap can be changed without changing the data. Stack two panels with sharex=True, or plot the ratio itself.
  3. Rewrite the title "Monthly online revenue, 2024 and 2025" as a finding, and say what you checked first. Answer: For example "Online revenue beat 2024 in every month of 2025", after checking that the 2025 value is higher in all 12 months (it is; the smallest gap is December, $4,803).

Reading the tickets: question 1 answered with "a list" or with for ax in axs (which loops over rows of a 2-D array) needs the shape table again at the start of Session 10. For question 2 accept "the scales can be chosen" in any words; "it looks cluttered" misses the point. For question 3 the check matters more than the wording: a ticket with a headline and no check is the learner to talk to first next time.
