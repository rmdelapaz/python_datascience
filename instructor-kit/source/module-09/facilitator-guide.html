Session 9: Composing and Customizing Figures

How to teach the session where charts become figures for other people. Before the break, learners put several charts on one page: grids returned as arrays, shared scales chosen on purpose, uneven layouts drawn as a picture of names, and the twin-axis chart that can be made to say anything. After the break they edit one chart from Matplotlib's first draft to a finished figure, one decision at a time, and capture the repeated decisions in a house style.

The risk of Session 9: charts that persuade instead of inform

Both lessons are about the same power: the person who draws the chart decides what the reader sees first. Separate y scales make a small store look as big as a large one. A twin axis can make two lines hug or fly apart without changing a number. A finding title can claim more than the data shows; a cut-off bar axis can quadruple a difference. Write the two questions on the board and come back to them all session: "Could I redraw this to tell a different story without changing the data?" and "Did I check the headline with a number?"

Known environment traps

Small screens. A 3 by 3 grid at figsize=(9, 6.5) is unreadable on a laptop at 125% zoom in a narrow notebook pane. If learners squint, have them widen the pane or drop to figsize=(8, 6); do not shrink fonts.

tight_layout from old habits. Calling fig.tight_layout() on a figure made with layout="constrained" prints a warning that the layout engine changed. Use one or the other; this course uses constrained.

Fonts. Text on the projector machine and on learner machines may use different fonts. DejaVu Sans ships with Matplotlib and is always available; nobody needs to install anything.

Charts in VS Code cells vs files. The kit labs save PNGs and never call plt.show(). Run them cell by cell in VS Code to see each figure, or open the files in output/.

Warm-up: Retrieval from Session 8

10 min
How to run it

Closed notebooks. Read each question aloud, 30 seconds of silent writing, one answer from the room, then yours. Questions 4 and 5 are today's foundation: if the room hesitates on Figure versus Axes, draw the page-and-panels picture now, before the 3.2 frame.

Five retrieval questions (with answers)
  1. Before you build a feature table, what is the first thing you decide? Answer: The unit of analysis: what one row represents. Every feature must be one fact about that unit.
  2. After a left merge of per-customer summaries, which NaN values become 0 and which stay missing? Answer: Counts and sums become 0 (true statements). Averages, shares and recency stay missing; add a flag such as has_ordered.
  3. State the cutoff rule. Answer: For every feature: could I have computed this value on the cutoff date, using only what existed then? If not, it leaks.
  4. What is the difference between a Figure and an Axes? Answer: The Figure is the whole canvas, the thing that gets saved. An Axes is one chart on it, with its own x and y axis.
  5. ax.bar(daily["store"], daily["revenue"]) draws four bars and no error. What are their heights? Answer: Each store's single highest day: 2,903 bars pile up at four positions and the tallest shows. ax.bar never aggregates; group first.

Subplots and Composition

75 min in the room
Objectives (the lesson's own)
  • Create a grid of Axes with plt.subplots(nrows, ncols) and reach each one by index or in a loop
  • Decide when panels should share an x or y axis, and explain what the reader loses when they do not
  • Build an uneven layout with plt.subplot_mosaic() and address its panels by name
  • Explain why a chart with two different y scales can mislead, and replace it with stacked panels or a ratio
  • Draw small multiples from grouped data with one function and one loop

The mental model to say

Say: "Think of a newspaper's front page. The page is one object, divided into panels, and the arrangement tells you how to read them: panels in a row invite comparison, a wide panel across the top is the headline, smaller panels underneath are the detail. In Matplotlib the page is the Figure and each panel is an Axes. You already draw on an Axes. Today is about arranging them."

Draw the lesson's decision on the board: Same chart for each group? Yes → a grid (plt.subplots), and share the axes so sizes compare. No, a mix → a layout (subplot_mosaic), with the biggest panel for the main point.

Say: "With several Axes there is no single 'current chart' any more. That is why we have held ax in a variable since the first chart."

Three mistakes learners will make, and how to coach each
  • Plotting on the array. axs.plot(...) raises AttributeError: 'numpy.ndarray' object has no attribute 'plot'; so does axs[0].plot(...) on a 2-D grid, because axs[0] is a whole row. Coach: "Print type(axs) and axs.shape. Which single panel do you mean?" Then axs[0, 0] or a loop over axs.flat.
  • Two indexes on a one-row grid. axs[0, 1] after plt.subplots(1, 3) raises IndexError: too many indices for array: array is 1-dimensional, but 2 were indexed. Coach with the shape table: one row comes back 1-D. Fix: axs[1], or squeeze=False when the grid size varies.
  • Comparing heights across unshared panels, or a pyplot call inside the loop. Neither raises anything: the reader concludes the stores are the same size, or three panels lose their titles. Coach: "Is size the point of this figure? Then sharey=True." And: "Which Axes does plt.title write on?"

Customizing Plots

The mental model to say

Say: "The charts you made so far were for you: quick pictures to find out what the data says. A chart for someone else has a different job. It does not ask 'what is in here?'. It says 'here is what I found'."

Say: "Think of Matplotlib's default output as a first draft from a reporter: accurate, complete, and with no idea what the story is. Your job is the editor's: write the headline, cut what does not serve the story, and make sure the eye lands on the important part first. Customizing is editing, not decorating. Every change today answers one question: does this help the reader get the point faster?"

Point at the second board question: Did I check the headline with a number? "A finding title is a claim. Claims can be wrong."

  1. The first draft (3m). Say: "Two helper functions, then Matplotlib's default chart. Look at it the way a stranger would." 
    import matplotlib as mpl
import matplotlib.dates as mdates
from matplotlib.ticker import FuncFormatter

daily = pd.read_csv("data/daily_sales.csv", parse_dates=["date"])
daily["month"] = daily["date"].dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp()
monthly = daily.pivot_table(index="month", columns="store", values="revenue",
                            aggfunc="sum")

    The error appears when the figure is drawn, in a notebook at the end of the cell, far from the line that caused it. Matplotlib passes each tick by name, as x: the fix is "${x:,.0f}".
Three mistakes learners will make, and how to coach each
  • A format string without x. ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter("${:,.0f}") raises IndexError: tuple index out of range, and only when the figure is drawn, at the end of the cell. Coach: "Matplotlib passes each tick value by name. What is the name?" Fix: "${x:,.0f}". The neighbor error: ValueError: The number of FixedLocator locations (3) ... does not match the number of labels (2) from set_xticks with too few labels.
  • A finding title that overstates. No error. "Footwear is returned twice as often as anything else" when Apparel is at 9.3%; "returns are rising" from a wobbling line. Coach: "Which number on the chart proves that sentence?" When the headline overstates, edit the headline, not the data.
  • Decorating instead of editing. More colors, a thicker frame, a legend kept "just in case", a red and green pair, a bar axis cut to "show the difference". Coach with the checklist: "If you removed this, would the reader lose anything? Does color carry the message alone?" Bars start at zero; color needs a second cue.

Exit ticket (3 questions, with answers)
