Session 2: Vectorized Operations and Broadcasting

How to teach the session where learners stop writing loops over numbers. In the first half they describe what they want done to a whole array: arithmetic between parallel arrays, boolean masks that count, filter and combine, np.where, and summaries along an axis. In the second half they learn the two short rules that let NumPy combine a table with a row, a column or a single number, and the habit, checking shapes, that catches the mistakes NumPy will not report. By the end they have compared four stores of very different sizes fairly, in one line.

How to Use This Guide

  • Same rhythm for both lessons: frame (one mental model, the rules on the board) → live demo (you type, they type along) → guided lab (two kit labs per lesson: new questions on the same data) → start the lesson's own project. The lesson exercises, quiz and journal are homework.
  • Predict before every run. Both lessons are about being able to say what will come out: how many values, what shape, error or no error. In 1.3, write the two shapes on the board, right-aligned, before every operation, and take the room's call.
  • Every code block below was run to produce the output printed under it, on the course data with NumPy 2.5. Type it live; read errors from the last line.
  • The lessons are the reference. Keep numpy_vectorized_operations.html and numpy_broadcasting.html open for the diagrams (the mask as punched cardboard, the axis picture, the stamp, the right-aligned shape examples). Labs live in labs/module-02/.
  • Teal blocks are hands-on, amber blocks are watch-outs. Lesson codes (1.2, 1.3) match the course site.
The #1 risk of Session 2: wrong answers that raise no error

Until now a mistake produced a traceback. Today's important mistakes do not. Aggregating along the wrong axis gives numbers that answer a different question. A missing value drops silently out of both halves of a comparison. A row meeting a column builds a grid of sixteen plausible-looking numbers where four were wanted. A learner who has not built the habit of checking will leave thinking everything worked. So make checking the visible routine of the session: before a run, say the expected shape or count out loud; after it, print .shape or add the pieces up. Praise a learner who catches a wrong shape more than one who types fast. The second risk is pace: Lesson 1.2 is the longest in the module and it is easy to spend the whole first half on masks. Hold the 1.2 demo to 30 minutes; the lab is where masks stick.

Five questions (answers for you)
  1. Name the three rules that are true of every NumPy array and not of a Python list. Answer: One type for the whole array (the dtype), a fixed size, and operations that act on every element at once.
  2. What does [1, 2, 3] * 2 give? What does np.array([1, 2, 3]) * 2 give? Answer: [1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3] (a list repeats) and [2 4 6] (an array does arithmetic).
  3. b = a[1:4], then b[0] = 0. What happened to a, and how do you prevent it? Answer: a[1] is now 0, because a slice is a view. b = a[1:4].copy() makes an independent array.
  4. The mean of the revenue array printed nan. Why, and what did you use instead? Answer: Six values are missing and arithmetic with a missing value gives nan. np.nanmean(revenue) skips them.
  5. t has shape (7, 4): days by stores. What does t[:, 2] select, and what is its shape? Answer: Every row of column 2: one store's value on each of the seven days. Shape (7,).

Mental model to say

Say: "A Python loop is a craftsperson at a workbench: pick up one item, inspect it, work on it, put it down, pick up the next. Careful, flexible, slow. A vectorized operation is an assembly line. You set the machine up once, 'multiply by 1.08', and the whole batch flows through. The machine can be fast because every item on the belt is the same kind of thing: that is the one-dtype rule from last session."

Then the goal for today, in the lesson's words: "By the end, when someone asks 'which days beat $15,000, and what was the average on sale weekends?', your first thought is an array expression and not a for statement."

Write three lines on the board and leave them up: The sum of a mask counts; its mean is a fraction. · **&, |, ~, with parentheses around every comparison. · The axis you name is the axis that disappears.**

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • **and / or, or no parentheses.** Symptoms: ValueError: The truth value of an array with more than one element is ambiguous, or TypeError: ufunc 'bitwise_and' not supported for the input types. Coach: "Read me the last line. Which word in your line wants a single True or False?" For the second: "What does Python do first here, the & or the >? Put a pair of parentheses around each question." Do not type the fix for them; point at board rule two.
  • The wrong axis, with no error. Symptom: 56 numbers where 4 were expected (or the reverse), or a plausible answer to a different question. Coach: "How many answers should there be: one per store or one per day? Print .shape of what you got." Then: "Which axis has to disappear to leave that many?" In Lab B make every learner say the expected shape before running each aggregation.
  • **Forgetting that nan is in neither group.** Symptom: counts that do not add up to 2,903; a mean of nan after filtering revenue; (revenue == np.nan).sum() giving 0. Coach: "How many store-days are there? How many are in your two groups? Where are the other six?" The habit: any time revenue is involved, ask what happens to the six missing values, and use np.isnan and the nan-aware functions on purpose.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab (cold-call gently, or think-pair-share):

  1. big = revenue > 15000. What is big.sum()? What is revenue[big].sum()? Answer: The first counts the store-days above $15,000 (101). The second adds up their revenue.
  2. How do I write "Flagstaff or Bend" as a mask? Answer: (stores == "Flagstaff") | (stores == "Bend"), or np.isin(stores, ["Flagstaff", "Bend"]).
  3. A table has shape (56, 4). What is the shape of table.mean(axis=0), and what does it hold? Answer: (4,): one average per store. The 56 days were collapsed.
  4. picked = week[week > 9000], then picked[0] = 0. Did week change? Answer: No. Selecting with a mask builds a new array. Assigning through the mask, week[week > 9000] = 0, would change it.
  5. When is a Python loop still fine? Answer: Over a handful of groups (four stores, twelve months), or when each step truly depends on the previous result. Not over the rows.

Mental model to say

Say: "Picture a paper form with seven lines and a rubber stamp that prints one line of four numbers. To stamp the whole form you press it on line 1, then line 2, down the page. The stamp does not get longer. It is reused. Broadcasting is NumPy doing the stamping: when one array is missing a dimension, or has length 1 along it, NumPy reuses it along that dimension."

The two rules, written on the board as a procedure learners will do on paper all session: 1. Write the shapes one above the other, lined up on the RIGHT. A missing entry counts as 1. 2. Each pair must be equal, or one of them is 1. The result takes the larger of each pair. Any pair that fails: the "could not be broadcast together" error.

And the pattern to remember, under the rules: **per column (per store): a table and a row, it just works. Per row (per day): a table and a column, so keepdims=True.**

Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • A flat array where a column was needed. Symptom: ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together with shapes (224,4) (224,). This is the friendly one. Coach: "The message gives you both shapes. Write them one above the other, right-aligned. Which pair fails?" Then: "What shape would work? Which keyword keeps that axis?" Resist saying keepdims first.
  • The accidental grid. Symptom: no error; a result with far more values than expected, or a summary number that is wildly off (an average error of 1,753 when every forecast was within about 250). Coach: "How many answers should there be? Print .shape." Then the board procedure on (4,) and (4, 1). Lab B Step 4 is built to produce this; let pairs be puzzled for a minute before you step in.
  • The square table that hides a wrong axis. Symptom: no error and a right-shaped result that is simply wrong, only when rows and columns happen to be equal in number. Coach: "Check it: if each row was centered on its own mean, what should each row add up to?" The durable fix is a habit, not a patch: keepdims=True on every axis=1 aggregation that is combined with its table, and a check line such as share.sum(axis=1).

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab (cold-call gently, or think-pair-share):

  1. Shapes (5, 3) and (5,). Does it broadcast? Answer: No. From the right, 3 meets 5: not equal, neither is 1. The five values need to be a column, (5, 1).
  2. I want each store's column minus that store's mean. Do I need keepdims? Answer: No. Per-column values are a row of shape (4,), which lines up with the table's last axis as it stands.
  3. What does a column of shape (3, 1) times a row of shape (5,) give? Answer: A (3, 5) table of every combination.
  4. Name the three ways to turn a flat array of n values into a column. Answer: arr[:, np.newaxis], arr.reshape(-1, 1), or keepdims=True on the aggregation that made it.
  5. Does broadcasting copy the small array seven times before subtracting? Answer: No. The small array is reused from the same memory. Only the result is full size.

Closing

Exit ticket (3 questions, no notes, 4 minutes)

Three questions on the slide, answered on a sticky note or in the workbook's Exit Ticket box. Question 3 is the one to sort by: a learner who writes keepdims=True and can say why has the session.

  1. revenue and promo are parallel arrays. Write one line that counts the sale days (promo equal to 1) with revenue above 10,000. Answer: ((revenue > 10000) & (promo == 1)).sum(): &, parentheses around each comparison, and the sum of the mask counts.
  2. A table has shape (224, 4): days by stores. What is the shape of table.mean(axis=0), and what does each value mean? Answer: (4,): one mean per store. The axis you name (the 224 days) is the one that disappears.
  3. You want every row of that table divided by its own row total. Write the expression, and say what goes wrong if you leave out the keyword. Answer: table / table.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True). Without keepdims=True the totals have shape (224,); from the right, 4 meets 224 and NumPy raises "could not be broadcast together".
