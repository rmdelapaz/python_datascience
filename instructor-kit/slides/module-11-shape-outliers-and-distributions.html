4.2

Shape, Position and Outliers

Shape is the first thing to check.

4.2 · Mental model

A pile of sand

  • Pour every value of a column onto a number line
  • The pile is the distribution; a histogram is a photograph of it
  • Center: where the pile sits. Spread: how wide it is
  • Shape: one mound, a mound with a ramp, or two mounds
  • Position: where one value sits in the pile
WHY Every summary is an attempt to describe the pile without showing the photograph.

Two outlier rules, two answers

q1, q3 = totals.quantile(0.25), totals.quantile(0.75)

flag_z = z_totals.abs() > 3

WHY The mean and SD are inflated by the values being judged. The quartiles are not.
4.2 · Judgment

A flag is a question, not a verdict

  1. Provable error? Fix it or remove it, and note what you did
  2. Real, and part of your question? Keep it. Use the median and IQR
  3. Real, but a different kind of thing? Analyze separately, and say so
  4. Never: silently delete whatever a rule flags
WHY The 12 largest orders: completed, online, one line of 25 to 39 units. Outfitters. Real revenue.

4.2 · Reading

A box plot is the IQR rule, drawn

  • The line in the box is the median, not the mean
  • The box runs from Q1 to Q3: the middle half
  • Each whisker ends at the last data point inside the fence
  • Separate points are the flagged values: candidates, nothing more
  • Compares groups well. Hides humps
TRAP Missing values give an empty frame and no error: dropna() first.

4.3

Probability Distributions

You cannot know tomorrow's number. You can know how it behaves.

4.3 · Mental model

The vending machine

  • Press the button: a random snack. You cannot say which
  • But you know the stocking list: 50% chips, 30% candy, 20% cookies
  • So you know what to expect, and how surprised to be
  • That stocking list is a probability distribution
  • Discrete: counted. Continuous: measured
WHY Every later tool asks: what would chance alone produce?
4.3 · Code

Simulate: count instead of calculating

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)              # ONE generator, created once

many = rng.integers(1, 7, size=100_000)      # 100,000 rolls of a die
shares = pd.Series(many).value_counts(normalize=True).sort_index()
print(shares.round(3).tolist())
print("mean of the rolls:", round(many.mean(), 3))
[0.167, 0.167, 0.165, 0.167, 0.168, 0.166]
mean of the rolls: 3.5
WHY The average settles on the expected value, 3.5. No single roll is ever 3.5.
4.3 · Code

Freeze a normal, check it against the data

spend_model = stats.norm(loc=mu, scale=sigma)       # scale is the SD
print("mean:", round(mu, 2), " SD:", round(sigma, 2))

for k in (1, 2, 3):
    in_data = ((spend - mu).abs() < k * sigma).mean()
    in_theory = stats.norm.cdf(k) - stats.norm.cdf(-k)
    print(f"within {k} SD:  data {in_data:.1%}   normal curve {in_theory:.1%}")
mean: 20.87  SD: 3.0
within 1 SD:  data 68.6%   normal curve 68.3%
within 2 SD:  data 95.1%   normal curve 95.4%
within 3 SD:  data 99.6%   normal curve 99.7%
WHY 68-95-99.7 is a property of the normal shape. Order totals would not follow it.
4.3 · The interface

Four questions, four methods

  • cdf(x): how likely is x or less?
  • sf(x): how likely is more than x?
  • ppf(q): which value has probability q below it?
  • pmf(k): how likely is exactly k? (counts only)
  • rvs(size, random_state=rng): random draws
WHY Say the question in words first. Then pick the method.

4.3 · Choosing

Which distribution?

  • Equally likely anywhere in a range: uniform. Check: flat histogram
  • A sum or average of many small effects: normal. Check: 68-95-99.7
  • Successes out of n tries: binomial. Check: independent, same p
  • Events in an interval: Poisson. Check: mean about equal to variance
WHY A model's assumptions are part of the model.

4.3 · Code

One sample, a standard error, an interval

sample = rng.choice(population, size=200, replace=False)
x_bar, s = sample.mean(), sample.std(ddof=1)
se = s / np.sqrt(len(sample))
low, high = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(sample) - 1, loc=x_bar, scale=se)
print("sample mean:", round(x_bar, 2), " sample SD:", round(s, 2))
print("standard error:", round(se, 2),
      " scipy.stats.sem:", round(stats.sem(sample), 2))
print(f"95% CI: {low:.2f} to {high:.2f}")
print("true mean:", round(population.mean(), 2))
sample mean: 182.93  sample SD: 147.89
standard error: 10.46  scipy.stats.sem: 10.46
95% CI: 162.31 to 203.55
true mean: 194.6
WHY SD: how much single orders vary. SE: how much the sample mean would vary.
4.3 · Interpretation

What "95% confident" means

  • The 95% describes the method, not this one interval
  • Build 100 intervals from 100 samples: about 95 contain the true mean
  • Any single interval either contains it or does not
  • It is not a range that holds 95% of the orders
  • Half the width costs four times the data
WHY Inside one study you cannot tell which kind of interval you drew.
4.3 · Common mistakes

Three that raise no error

  • scale=sigma ** 2: the variance where scipy wants the SD
  • sf(5) for "5 or more"; ppf where cdf was meant
  • A new default_rng(42) inside the loop: the same draw every time
  • Reading a confidence interval as a range for the data
WATCH Name the arguments: loc=, scale=. Create the generator once.
